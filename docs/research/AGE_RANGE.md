# Omramo age-range research

**Date:** 13 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Answer Srikanth's question (t3736u) and inform PRD Q2 (exact ages per tier). This note recommends a range; it does not close Q2 until Srikanth agrees.  
**Scope:** Omramo is an English story reader for children who can already read independently. There is no read-aloud control. The question is when children start *hearing* moral stories, when they can *read* them alone, when they move from heavily illustrated books to chapter books and then to text-dominant “real” books, and what age band that implies for the app.

No figures below are invented. Where a national standard does not exist (for example, official words-per-page by grade), that is stated and the guidance is compared with publisher formats only.

## Short answer (recommended for Q2)

| Question | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| When do kids start *hearing* moral stories? | As preschool read-alouds, typically from about ages 3–5, and formally in early school (UK Year 1 includes fairy stories and traditional tales). They often enjoy the plot long before they can state the moral. |
| When do they start *reading* them alone? | Once independent reading is in place: typically ages **6–8** (US grades 1–2, UK Year 2 to the start of Year 3). Extracting the moral without help is later, often around **9–10**, unless an adult or the app prompts them to explain. |
| Recommended Omramo starting age | **About 7**, with strong 6-year-olds who already read English independently welcome in Low. Not for pre-readers or the Apple “5 and under” band. |
| Cartoonish / picture-heavy → chapter books | Typically **ages 7–9** (US grades 2–3; UK Years 3–4). |
| Chapter books → text-dominant “legit” books | Typically **ages 8–12**, solidifying around **9–11** as middle-grade novels take over and pictures recede. |
| Overall Omramo range | **Ages 6–12**, with the core audience **7–11** (US grades 2–5; UK Years 2–6). Low is the on-ramp; Advanced sits at the start of text-dominant reading, not at young-adult novels. |

## (a) Hearing moral stories versus reading them alone

These are two different milestones.

**Hearing them (read-aloud) starts early.** Nemours KidsHealth notes that by age 3 children usually listen to longer read-alouds and can retell a familiar story, and that kindergarteners (about age 5) can retell a main idea and put events in order. The England national curriculum requires Year 1 pupils (ages 5–6) to become “very familiar with key stories, fairy stories and traditional tales,” including books above the level they can read independently; Year 2 continues a “wider range of stories, fairy stories and traditional tales,” again including works read *to* them as well as books they can read themselves.

**Reading them independently comes later,** after decoding and fluency. Jeanne Chall’s stages place initial decoding at about ages 6–7 (grades 1–2) and confirmation and fluency at about ages 7–8 (grades 2–3). Harvard GSE’s literacy benchmarks, drawn from Blatt, Lesaux and Snow, say that at 6 a child “starts to read words on the page,” at 7 “starts to read words automatically,” and at 8 “reads chapter books.” Nemours KidsHealth places “read longer books independently” in second and third grade (ages 7–8). In England, by the beginning of Year 3 (ages 7–8) pupils “should be able to read books written at an age-appropriate interest level… accurately and at a speed that is sufficient for them to focus on understanding what they read.”

**Grasping the moral is later still, and usually needs a prompt.** Walker and Lombrozo (2017), in *Cognition*, write that “the ability to spontaneously extract ‘the moral’ of a story develops relatively late,” and that young children tend to represent stories at a concrete, surface level. Their experiments show that 5- and 6-year-olds *can* abstract and generalise a moral when they are prompted to explain parts of the story — sometimes more effectively than if they are simply told the moral. Common Sense Media researcher Seeta Pai, quoted by NPR (2015), said the ability to articulate a moral theme “develops fairly slowly, emerging only around age 9 or 10” in US research. Harvard GSE’s benchmark at age 10 is that a child “begins to identify the themes in a text.”

**Implication for Omramo:** the app should not wait until age 9 to *offer* moral folktales — children hear and can read simpler ones earlier — but Low and Medium should not assume the child will extract the moral unaided. A short, child-facing reflection prompt (already raised as CQ6 / Q27 in the content plan) is supported by the Walker and Lombrozo finding that explanation helps 5- and 6-year-olds get there.

Sources: [Nemours KidsHealth, Reading Milestones](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/milestones.html); [England national curriculum, English programmes of study](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study); [Landmark Outreach summary of Chall, 1983](https://www.landmarkoutreach.org/strategies/challs-stages-reading-development/); [Harvard GSE, Raising Strong Readers](https://www.gse.harvard.edu/ideas/usable-knowledge/16/03/raising-strong-readers); [Walker and Lombrozo, 2017, PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt7df9v6th/qt7df9v6th.pdf); [NPR, 8 January 2015](https://www.npr.org/2015/01/08/375878511/and-the-moral-of-the-story-is-kids-dont-always-understand-the-moral).

## (b) Recommended Omramo starting age

**About 7 years old** (US grade 2; UK Year 2; around Indian Grade 2), which is when independent reading of short, illustrated stories is typical — not merely the start of phonics.

A strong 6-year-old who already reads English independently can start in Low. The product should not be positioned for preschoolers or for children who still need a read-aloud, because the governing rule is that the child reads the words and there is no read-aloud control.

Why not 5, and why not later than 7?

- Age 5 (US kindergarten / UK Reception–Year 1) is still largely Chall Stage 0–1: matching letters and sounds, reading simple words, and being read *to*. That is the picture-book and shared-reading stage, not Omramo’s job.
- Age 6 is when many children *begin* to read words on the page (Harvard GSE). The very first independent books — early / easy readers — use tightly controlled vocabulary, very short sentences, and few characters (Boyds Mills). Omramo Low, at about 55 words a page with world-folktale names and places, is richer than a typical “I Can Read” Level 1 page, so it fits better once decoding is becoming automatic (late grade 1 / grade 2).
- By 7–8, fluency is the instructional focus (Chall Stage 2; US grades 2–3; UK Year 2 into Year 3). That is the natural on-ramp for an illustration-driven independent reader.

In India, the official target is later: NIPUN Bharat aims for proficiency in foundational literacy and numeracy **for every child by Grade 3**, with learning outcomes framed for ages 3–9. For many Indian children, especially those learning to read English as an additional language, independent English reading may arrive closer to age 8–9. The app’s *starting ability* should stay “already reads English independently,” not a fixed birthday.

Sources: as in (a), plus [Boyds Mills on early readers versus picture books](https://boydsmills.org/blog/picture-book-early-reader-chapter-book-where-does-your-story-fit/); [Government of India, PIB, NIPUN Bharat, 29 November 2021](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1776158).

## (c) The two transitions: “cartoonish” books to chapter books, then to “legit” books

Publishers treat these as formats, not birthdays, and the age bands overlap. The useful distinctions are who is meant to read the book, how much the pictures carry the meaning, and how long and complex the prose is.

| Format | Typical reader age (publisher guidance) | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Picture books | About 2–8 (CANSCAIP; often read *to* the child) | Words and pictures tell the story together. Boyds Mills: designed to be read *to* a child, not by the child. |
| Early / easy readers | Roughly 5–7, overlapping early primary | Designed to be read *by* the child. Short sentences, large type, full-colour pictures that support the words, few named characters. Example: a 32-page I Can Read title of about 567 words (Boyds Mills). |
| Chapter books (including first / transitional chapter books) | CANSCAIP: **7–9**, 5,000–15,000 words. Penguin / Puffin: typically **5–10**, up to about 20,000 words | Prose starts to carry the story. Pictures shrink to spot art or chapter openers; some spreads have no art. Type is smaller, sentences may run across pages. First-chapter-book lines exist because the jump from a 600-word early reader to an 8,000-word chapter book is large. |
| Middle grade | Widely **8–12** (Publishers Weekly; CANSCAIP; Penguin: 25,000–50,000 words; SCBWI *The Book*, as cited by Kite Tales: 8–12, 25,000–62,500 words) | Independent, confident readers. Longer plots, fewer or no illustrations. This is what parents often mean by “actual books.” |

**Transition 1 — early readers / picture-heavy books → chapter books:** typically **ages 7–9**, most often grades 2–3. Harvard GSE: at 8, “reads chapter books.” Nemours: ages 7–8, “read longer books independently.” In a UK trade summary of curriculum expectations, Year 3 (ages 7–8) is described as the move from learning to read to reading to learn, with longer books and more independence; Year 4 (ages 8–9) is when “most children are expected to read chapter books independently.” Penguin describes chapter books as the stepping stone toward middle grade.

**Transition 2 — chapter books → text-dominant middle-grade novels:** typically **ages 8–12**, becoming reliable around **9–11** (US grades 4–5, UK Years 5–6). Chall’s Stage 3, “reading for learning the new,” begins at about ages 8–14 / grades 4–8. Harvard GSE: from grade 4, children are expected to “read to learn.” Publishers Weekly records the trade definition of middle grade as books for readers eight to 12, and a Scholastic editor distinguishing chapter books (building confidence) from middle grade (already independent, confident readers). Illustrations may remain in some middle-grade titles, especially for younger readers, but they are no longer required to follow the story (Penguin).

There is no single birthday when “cartoonish” books stop. The range Omramo should cover is exactly this span: late early-reader / first chapter book at Low, through illustrated chapter book at Medium, to the doorway of text-dominant middle grade at Advanced — not YA novels.

Sources: [CANSCAIP, Children’s Book Categories](https://www.canscaip.org/Childrens-Book-Categories); [Boyds Mills, Fancy Nancy format comparison](https://boydsmills.org/blog/picture-book-early-reader-chapter-book-where-does-your-story-fit/); [Penguin / Puffin, How to write a children’s middle grade book](https://www.penguin.co.uk/about/company-articles/how-to-write-a-children-s-middle-grade-book); [Publishers Weekly, Navigating Middle Grade Books](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/childrens/childrens-industry-news/article/76625-navigating-middle-grade.html); [SCBWI Kite Tales, word count, citing *The Book*](https://scbwikitetales.com/2022/01/19/ask-an-editor-word-count/); [Reading Chest, UK year-group expectations](https://readingchest.co.uk/advice/reading-levels-uk/); Harvard GSE, Nemours, and Chall as above.

## (d) Recommended overall range and Low / Medium / Advanced mapping

**Overall range: ages 6–12, core 7–11.**  
That is early-to-middle grades, which already matches the PRD’s wording. It starts when independent reading is typical and ends at the upper edge of middle grade, before young adult (about 12 and up in SCBWI / CANSCAIP guidance).

Approximate grade equivalents: US grades 1–6; England Years 2–6 (Key Stage 1 end through Key Stage 2); India Grades 2–5/6, with the national foundational-literacy goal at Grade 3.

Lexile figures below use two official MetaMetrics scales, which must not be mixed up:

- **Student norms** (2021–2025, about 20 million US students): how actual students score. Spring 50th percentiles are Grade 1 110L, Grade 2 335L, Grade 3 535L, Grade 4 710L, Grade 5 860L, Grade 6 980L. A child at the 50th percentile may still be below a state’s “proficient” standard.
- **CCSS “stretch” text ranges** (college- and career-readiness text demand, not typical student scores): Grade 1 190L–530L; Grades 2–3 420L–820L; Grades 4–5 740L–1010L; Grades 6–8 925L–1185L.

Omramo is pleasure reading of folktales, not test prep. Tier targets should sit near typical student measures (and a reader’s “sweet spot” of about 100L below to 50L above their measure), not automatically at the top of the stretch bands.

Fountas & Pinnell instructional grade-level equivalents from Heinemann’s Benchmark chart: Grade 1 E–J; Grade 2 K–M; Grade 3 N–P; Grade 4 Q–S; Grade 5 T–V; Grade 6 W–Y.

**Words per page:** there is no official US, UK or Indian words-per-page standard by grade. The current PRD targets (Low ~55, Medium ~90, Advanced ~190) are product choices. They compare reasonably with formats: a 32-page early reader of ~567 words is much thinner per page than Low, once art-only pages are included; a 124-page chapter book of ~8,700 words is about 70 words a page, close to Low/Medium; a novel page is often well above 200 words, so Advanced at ~190 is still slightly short of a dense “real book” page, which fits an illustration-aware app.

| Tier | Recommended ages | Typical grade | Approx. F&P | Approx. Lexile (student-comfortable, not stretch-ceiling) | Words per page | Reading type |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **6–8**, typical start **7** | US 1–2 (mainly late 1 and 2); UK Y2–Y3; India G2–G3 | **I–M** | **about 200L–500L** (around spring Grade 1–2 norms, into early Grade 3) | Keep **about 40–70**; current **55** is appropriate | Early-reader to first chapter book: short sentences, few characters, large type, art on the facing pane carrying new words and places |
| **Medium** | **8–10** | US 3–4; UK Y3–Y5; India G3–G5 | **N–S** | **about 500L–800L** (around spring Grade 3–4 norms) | Keep **about 80–120**; current **90** is appropriate | Illustrated chapter book: longer sentences, a bigger idea, still one text pane |
| **Advanced** | **10–12** | US 5–6; UK Y5–Y6; India G5–G6 | **T–Y** | **about 800L–1000L** (around spring Grade 5–6 norms; do not push into the 6–8 stretch ceiling of 1185L) | Keep **about 160–220**; current **190** is appropriate | Doorway to text-dominant middle grade: text on both panes, deeper theme, still not a bare novel |

Tiers should stay ability bands, not hard age locks. The PRD already lets a child open any tier. A fluent 8-year-old may read Advanced; a 10-year-old still consolidating English may prefer Low.

Sources: [Lexile Grade Level Charts (student norms)](https://hub.lexile.com/lexile-grade-level-charts/); [MetaMetrics Lexile map, CCSS text ranges](https://www2.metametricsinc.com/hubfs/Lexile-Map-for-Reading_11x17.pdf?hsLang=en); [Heinemann F&P grade-level equivalence chart](https://www.heinemann.com/shared/supportingmaterials/benchmark/gradelevelequivalencechart.pdf); PRD tier typography and words-per-page (current product); format comparisons in (c).

## (e) Implications: Apple Kids Category and current type sizes

### Kids Category age bands

Apple’s Kids category is for apps designed for children **ages 11 and under**. In App Store Connect the developer must choose **one** primary-audience band: **5 and under**, **6–8**, or **9–11**. The app must follow App Review Guidelines 1.3 and 5.1.4. It can also be discovered in another primary and secondary category (for example Education or Books).

- **Do not choose “5 and under.”** Omramo requires independent reading and has no read-aloud. That band is for pre-literate and emergent readers.
- The product’s real span (about 6–11/12) does not fit a single Apple band cleanly. The whole catalog in a Kids listing must be appropriate for the band you pick.
- **If every story, including Advanced, is written and themed so a typical 6-to-8-year-old can use the app safely,** choose **6–8**. That matches the independent-reading on-ramp and puts Omramo in front of families who are just leaving early readers. Deeper literary language can still live in Advanced, but war, death, slavery and similar material (already flagged in content-plan CQ9 / Q29) would need age-appropriate handling — or would push the listing up.
- **If Advanced keeps heavier historical or tragic themes that are not appropriate as a 6–8 primary experience,** choose **9–11**. That also matches the age at which children more reliably articulate a moral theme (around 9–10). Younger independent readers can still use Low, but Kids-category browse will present the app as 9–11.
- Either way, set Education or Books as the other category so families can find it outside the single Kids band. The App Store *age rating* (4+, 9+, 13+) is a separate control from the Kids band; stories that treat death or war may land at **9+** even if the Kids band is 6–8. That pairing should be checked when CQ9 is decided.

A practical recommendation, pending the sensitive-content decision: treat **6–8 as the preferred Kids band only if the catalog is held to 6–8 appropriateness**; otherwise **9–11**. Do not list in “5 and under.”

Sources: [Apple, App Store categories — Kids](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/categories/); [Apple, Design safe and age-appropriate experiences (Kids category, age bands, parental gates)](https://developer.apple.com/kids/).

### Do the current type sizes fit?

**Yes. They are slightly generous, which the evidence supports, and they fit screen reading.**

Current locked sizes: Low 23.5 / 37 pt; Medium 19.5 / 32 pt; Advanced 16.5 / 27 pt. Leading is about 1.57–1.64 times the type size.

- Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines give a default text size of **17 pt** (minimum 11 pt) on iOS and iPadOS. Advanced at 16.5 pt is essentially that default. Low and Medium are larger, which is appropriate for newer readers on a phone or folded Duo.
- Hughes and Wilkins (2000), *Journal of Research in Reading*, found that children aged **5–7 read fastest** on the larger typography designed for 5- and 6-year-olds, and that children aged 8–11 were neither clearly helped nor harmed by size — but children of all ages, especially those prone to visual stress, made **more errors on smaller text**. They concluded that some children would benefit from larger type and spacing than was then typical, “and that no children would be disadvantaged by such a change.” In the same paper they record Burt (1959) advocating **24 pt up to age 7**, decreasing to 11 pt over age 12. Low at 23.5 pt sits next to that 24 pt figure.
- Print middle-grade novels are often set smaller than 16.5 pt, but those sizes are for paper held at a desk distance, not a phone. There is no need to shrink Advanced to look more “grown up.” Keep the existing “Aa” control so older children can reduce size if they want, and so smaller text remains an option rather than a default.

Words-per-page targets can stay as they are (see the table in (d)). Low should be made easy by **sentence length and vocabulary**, not by cutting type size. Fifty-five large-type words of a world folktale are already a fuller page than many Level 1 early readers; if a later milestone wants to welcome the newest 6-year-old decoders, add a slightly thinner Low page (closer to 40 words) rather than a fourth tier.

Sources: [Apple HIG, Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography); [Hughes and Wilkins, 2000 (author PDF)](https://visualstress.info/2000-144.pdf).

## Devices (why iPhone and iPad both matter)

Omramo will often be used on a shared family iPad or iPhone at the young end, and on a child’s own device more often as they near 11.

- Common Sense Media’s 2025 Census (*Media Use by Kids Zero to Eight*): **40%** of children have their own tablet by age 2; **nearly 1 in 4** have a personal cellphone by age 8. Screen time in this age group is about 2.5 hours a day.
- Common Sense Media’s caregiver guide (reviewed 26 April 2024): **by age 11, about half of children in the US own a smartphone.**

That supports the already-decided universal app (iPhone, Duo, iPad) and Family Sharing: the buyer is the parent, and the youngest readers are more likely to be on an iPad than on their own phone.

Sources: [Common Sense, 2025 Census overview](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight); [Common Sense press release, 26 February 2025](https://about.commonsensemedia.org/press-room/press-releases/digital-childhood-starts-at-age-two-landmark-study-shows-evolution-of-young-childrens-media-use); [Common Sense, Cellphones and Devices guide](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/cellphones-and-devices-a-guide-for-parents-and-caregivers).

## UK and India, in brief

**England.** Year 1 (ages 5–6): phonics, and traditional tales mainly as listen-and-discuss. Year 2 (ages 6–7), end of Key Stage 1: accurate, fluent reading of age-appropriate books, still alongside being read to. Beginning of Year 3 (ages 7–8): read age-appropriate books accurately and fast enough to focus on meaning — the closest official English-curriculum marker for “ready for an independent reader app.” Chapter-book independence is widely described as settling in Years 3–4 (ages 7–9). Sources: [national curriculum](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-english-programmes-of-study); [Reading Chest year-group guide](https://readingchest.co.uk/advice/reading-levels-uk/).

**India.** NIPUN Bharat, launched 5 July 2021, sets the goal of foundational literacy and numeracy **for every child by Grade 3**, with development goals for ages 3–9. Independent reading, especially in English as an additional language, will often be later than the US/UK median. Omramo should keep an ability gate (“can already read this English”) rather than assume a Grade 1 English start worldwide. Source: [PIB, 29 November 2021](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1776158).

## What this does and does not decide

This note recommends, for PRD Q2:

- Starting age: **about 7**, with able 6-year-old independent readers welcome in Low.
- Overall range: **6–12**, core **7–11**.
- Tiers: Low 6–8 (typical 7), Medium 8–10, Advanced 10–12, as ability bands, not locks.
- Kids Category: never “5 and under”; choose **6–8** or **9–11** once sensitive-content policy (CQ9) is settled.
- Keep the current type sizes and words-per-page targets.

It does not set prices, change the no-read-aloud rule, or close Q2 on its own.
