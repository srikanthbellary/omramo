# Part I

# --- page 1
Long ago, the city of Novgorod stood rich and busy beside a river.

Boats crowded its wooden quays. Merchants in fur-trimmed coats counted their silver, and above the log roofs rose a white cathedral with domes like helmets.

Sadko counted no silver. He owned one thing in the world, a gusli of maple wood, and he could make its strings laugh or weep.

So the merchants sent for him whenever they feasted. He played until the candles burned low, and they paid him with his supper.

It was a thin living, but the music was his own.

# --- page 2
Then came a week when nobody sent for him. Not one door opened. Not one servant came running.

On the seventh day Sadko walked out of the city to the wide grey lake called Ilmen. He sat on a white stone and laid the gusli across his knees.

“If nobody will listen,” he said, “I will play for the water.”

He played from noon until the stars came out. And though no wind blew, the lake began to heave. Waves slapped the stone. Far out, something huge was rising.

# --- page 3
Up through the black water came a crown of shell and coral. Under it came a green beard as wide as a fishing net, streaming like a waterfall.

It was the Sea Tsar, king of every water from lake to ocean.

“I held a feast below,” he boomed, “and your playing was the best of it. I pay my debts, Sadko.”

“Tomorrow, tell the merchants there are fish with golden fins in Lake Ilmen. They will laugh. Wager with them. Then cast a silk net three times.”

The crown sank. The lake lay flat as a plate.

# --- page 4
The next evening Sadko was sent for at last. He sang the old songs of heroes. Then he laid his hands flat on the strings.

“There are fish with golden fins in Lake Ilmen,” he said.

The merchants roared with laughter. Sadko set his gusli on the table. “I wager this, which is all I have, against six shops full of goods.”

They rowed out by torchlight. Sadko cast the silk net once, twice, three times. Each time it came up with one fish, and each fish had fins of red gold.

By morning the musician was a merchant.

# --- page 5
Twelve good years went by. Sadko built thirty ships, and every one had a red sail.

In Novgorod, rivers were the roads. His ships carried furs, wax and honey down them and out to the open sea. They came home heavy with silver, cloth and salt.

He wore a coat trimmed with fur now, and he feasted in a hall of his own.

But the gusli hung on its peg, and dust lay along the strings. Sadko was too busy to play.

And in twelve years of sailing, he had never once thanked the Sea Tsar.

# Part II

# --- page 6
One night, far out at sea, all thirty ships stopped dead.

The wind was fair. The red sails stood full. Waves raced past on either side, yet the ships sat as if nailed to the water.

The sailors rolled a barrel of silver over the side. It sank, and nothing changed.

“It is not silver he wants,” said Sadko quietly. “It is me.”

He took down his dusty gusli. The sailors lowered an oak plank, and he stepped onto it. At once the ships leaped forward like horses let go, and the dark sea was empty.

# --- page 7
Sadko lay down on the plank, hugged the gusli and fell asleep to the rocking of the waves.

He woke on the floor of the sea. Green-gold light fell through the water. Before him stood a palace of white stone and shell, and fish swam in and out of its windows like swallows.

On a throne of coral sat the Sea Tsar, drumming his fingers.

“Twelve years you have sailed my sea,” he rumbled, “and paid me nothing. Not a coin. Not a tune. Now play!”

# --- page 8
Sadko played. The Sea Tsar hitched up his robe and danced. He stamped and spun, faster and faster, for a day and a night.

A quiet river-maiden slipped to Sadko’s side. Her name was Chernava.

“When he dances, the sea above us goes wild,” she whispered. “Ships are running for the harbour. The fishermen’s wives stand on the shore and count the sails.”

Sadko looked at his hands. Then he pulled, and every string snapped.

“My gusli is broken, great Tsar,” he said. “There is no mending it down here.”

# --- page 9
The Sea Tsar sat down with a bump that shook the palace. He was sorry, but he was not angry.

“Then stay,” he said. “Choose a bride from my daughters. You shall have a palace of pearl.”

The river-maidens glided past, bright with gold and sea-pearls. But Sadko had lived in golden rooms, and he had found them cold.

Last of all came Chernava, in plain grey. A small river near Novgorod had the very same name.

“I choose Chernava,” said Sadko. And he lay down to sleep in the green light.

# --- page 10
He woke with his cheek on wet grass. Beside him ran the little Chernava river, and beyond it the domes of Novgorod showed pale under the last stars of the night.

Round the bend of the Volkhov came a red sail. Then another. Then all thirty. The sailors shouted when they saw him waving from the bank.

Sadko never went to sea again. He strung his gusli with new strings.

And on still evenings he sat on the white stone by the lake and played for anyone who cared to listen, above the water or under it.
