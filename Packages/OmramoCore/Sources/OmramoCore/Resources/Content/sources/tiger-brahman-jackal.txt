# Part I

# --- page 1
A tiger sat in a cage beside a dusty road.

It was a trap of strong bamboo, set by a village in the Punjab, the land of five rivers. The Tiger had roared at the bars and bitten the bars. The bars had not minded at all.

Along the road came a poor Brahman, with a staff in his hand and a cloth bundle on his shoulder. “Holy one! Kind one!” cried the Tiger. “Open this door!”

“I think not, my friend,” said the Brahman mildly. “You would eat me.”

“Eat you? Never! I would carry your bundle for the rest of my days!”

# --- page 2
Now a Brahman is a priest and a teacher, and this one had a soft heart.

The Tiger wept. He swore by every stripe on his back that he would never harm so good a man. Great tears rolled down his nose.

So the Brahman lifted the latch. Out sprang the Tiger, not weeping now.

“What a simple man you are!” he purred. “I have sat in that box for days, and I am hollow. You shall be my dinner.”

“Is that fair?” said the Brahman, and his staff shook in his hand. “Let us ask the first three we meet.” The Tiger yawned and agreed.

# --- page 3
“Go and ask,” said the Tiger, stretching out in the dust. “I shall wait here. A Brahman always keeps his word.”

That was true, and a great nuisance. The Brahman walked until he came to an old pipal tree, whose heart-shaped leaves shivered in the smallest breeze.

“Pipal tree, I let a tiger out of a trap, and now he wishes to eat me. Is that fair?”

The leaves rustled crossly. “Fair? All day long travellers cool themselves under my leaves. Then they snap off my twigs for their goats and walk on. Do not come to me for thanks.”

One answer, and it was against him.

# --- page 4
Farther on, he heard a creak and a splash. An old buffalo was plodding round a well-wheel, and with every turn a row of clay pots tipped water into a ditch for the wheat.

The Brahman told his trouble a second time. “Is that fair, mother buffalo?”

The buffalo did not stop walking. “When I gave milk, they fed me the best seed cake and scratched my ears,” she said. “Now I am old, they tie me to this wheel and throw me scraps. Only a fool waits for thanks.”

Round she went again, and the pots went splash. Two answers, and both against him.

# --- page 5
There was nobody left to ask but the road itself, so the Brahman bent down and asked it.

The road answered in a dry, dusty whisper. “Feet tramp on me all day, and hooves, and bullock carts with squealing wheels. I carry every one of them to market and home again. What do they leave me? Dust, and whatever spills from their sacks.”

“Three answers,” said the Brahman, counting on his fingers, “and not one for me.”

He looked down the long pale road, which led away to anywhere he liked. Then he sighed, turned round, and started back to the Tiger. He had given his word.

# Part II

# --- page 6
Halfway back, a Jackal came trotting out of the mustard field.

“Good day, teacher!” said the Jackal. “Why so sad? You look like a kite with its string cut.”

So the Brahman told him everything: the cage, the tears, the promise, the tree, the buffalo and the road.

The Jackal scratched one ear. “How very muddling,” he said. “Would you say it again?” The Brahman said it again. The Jackal scratched the other ear.

“It is no use. My head is a basket with a hole in it. Take me to the place, and perhaps I shall see what you mean.”

# --- page 7
The Tiger was waiting by the cage, sharpening his claws on a thorn tree.

“The shadows have grown a good deal since you left,” he growled. “And who is this? I asked for one dinner, not two.”

“One moment more, my lord,” said the Brahman. “Only let me explain things to this jackal. It takes him a while to get hold of a thing.”

The Jackal bowed very low and looked as slow as he could.

So the Brahman told the tale once more. He told it slowly. He put in every bit of it, even the buffalo’s scraps, and the Tiger’s tail began to twitch.

# --- page 8
“My head is spinning like a potter’s wheel!” wailed the Jackal, clutching his ears. “Let me see. The Brahman sat in the cage, and down the road came my lord the Tiger…”

“No, you donkey!” roared the Tiger. “It was I who sat in the cage!”

“Of course! It was I who sat in the cage. No, that cannot be right. The cage sat in the bundle, and the bundle sat on the buffalo, and the buffalo lifted the latch…”

“Leave the buffalo out of it!” howled the Tiger.

“Then it is hopeless, my lord,” sighed the Jackal. “Eat your dinner. I shall never, never understand.”

# --- page 9
That was too much for the Tiger. “Then I shall show you!” he roared. “Use your eyes. Here stands the Tiger. There stands the Brahman. This is the cage. And the Tiger was inside it!”

“Tiger, Brahman, cage,” said the Jackal, pointing at the wrong one each time. “But inside, my lord? How could so grand a tiger fit through so small a door?”

“Like this, you fool!” The Tiger bounded into the cage and glared out through the bars. “Now do you see?”

“As clear as well water,” said the Jackal, and he dropped the latch. Clack!

# --- page 10
The Tiger stared at the bars. The bars still did not mind at all.

“There,” said the Jackal, dusting his paws. “Now everything is back where the village put it. I do like things tidy.”

The Brahman’s staff stopped shaking. He shouldered his bundle, and the two of them set off down the road together.

Behind them the Tiger sat down to think it over. At evening the villagers would come and carry the cage, tiger and all, deep into the forest, far from anybody’s goats.

The Brahman walked through the pipal’s shade with his eyes wide open, and beside him trotted the Jackal, grinning.
