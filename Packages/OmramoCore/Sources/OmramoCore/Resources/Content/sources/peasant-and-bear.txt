# Part I

# --- page 1
At the edge of a great forest lived a poor Peasant.

A peasant is a farmer who owns very little. This one had one field, one horse and one wooden plough.

One spring morning he went out to plough. The earth turned over, dark and soft, and the birds sang in the birch trees.

# --- page 2
Crack! went the bushes. Out of the forest came a great brown Bear.

“Peasant!” he roared. “This is my forest. I will break you up like a stick!”

The horse stopped. The birds stopped. But the Peasant did not run. He took off his cap.

# --- page 3
“Don’t, Bear,” he said. “Let us share. I will do the sowing. When the crop is ripe, you take the tops, and I will take only the roots.”

The Bear scratched his ear. Tops were on top. Tops must be best.

He did not ask what the Peasant was sowing. “Done!” he said, and he ambled back into the trees.

# --- page 4
Summer is short in the north, so the Peasant did not waste a day.

He sowed. He weeded. He chased away the crows.

Up came the leaves, thick and green. Now and then the Bear came by to look.

“My tops are growing nicely,” he said.

# --- page 5
Autumn came. The birch leaves turned yellow, and the Bear came for his share.

The Peasant took hold of some leaves and pulled. Pop! Out of the dark earth came a turnip, fat and white. Then another, and another.

He cut off the green tops and made a heap. “Yours, Bear. Just as we agreed.”

# --- page 6
The Bear sat down by his heap of leaves. He chewed one. It was tough. It was bitter.

The Peasant loaded his cart. In went the turnips, round as onions, until the cart was full.

The Bear stopped chewing. “What have you got there, Peasant?”

# --- page 7
“Only my roots, Bear. I am taking them to town to sell.”

“Give me one to try.”

The Peasant tossed him a turnip, and the Bear bit it in two. Crunch!

It was sweet. It was juicy. It was much, much nicer than leaves.

# --- page 8
“Grrr!” said the Bear. “Your roots are sweet and my tops are bitter. Next year, I take the roots!”

“As you wish, Bear,” said the Peasant. “Next year the roots are yours, and I will take only the tops.”

The Bear nodded hard. Once again, he did not ask what would be sown.

# Part II

# --- page 9
Next spring the Peasant ploughed his field again.

His wife carried out a sack of seed. “Turnips again?” she asked.

“Not this year,” said the Peasant. “This year we sow rye.” And he scattered the grain by the handful, left and right.

Far off in the trees, the Bear rubbed his paws. “My roots,” he said. “My sweet, sweet roots.”

# --- page 10
The rye came up green. All summer it grew, up to the Peasant’s knee, up to his belt, up to his shoulder.

Then it turned gold, and it whispered when the wind blew.

Every day the Bear came to look. “Grow, roots, grow,” he sang. He never once looked at the tops.

# --- page 11
At harvest time the Peasant came with his sickle, a knife curved like a new moon.

Swish, swish, swish! Down fell the golden rye. His wife tied it into sheaves, and they stacked the sheaves on the cart.

“Now, Bear,” said the Peasant, “the roots are yours. Every one.”

# --- page 12
The Bear dug. He pulled up a root, thin and dry as string. He chewed it. He pulled a face.

“Peasant!” he roared. “Tops, and I get leaves. Roots, and I get string! How do you do it?”

“I don’t do it, Bear. The crop does. Turnips grow down. Rye grows up. You never asked what I was sowing.”

# --- page 13
The Bear sat on his heap of roots and thought for a long time.

In the village, the Peasant’s wife baked black bread from the new rye. The whole house smelled of it.

Before the first snow, the Peasant carried a warm loaf and a basket of turnips to the edge of the forest. He left them on a stump.

# --- page 14
The Bear found them. He ate the loaf. He ate the turnips. He licked the basket.

“Next spring,” he said, with his mouth full, “I will not say tops, and I will not say roots. I will say: Peasant, what are we planting?”

Then he yawned a great yawn, and went off to sleep until spring.
