# Part I

# --- page 1
Sindbad of Baghdad had sworn never to go to sea again.

He had come home rich from his first voyage, and he was glad of dry land. He bought a house with a fountain in the courtyard. He gave feasts for his friends.

For a whole year he kept his promise. Then one morning he walked by the river, where the boats were unloading.

He smelled tar and wet rope. He heard sailors talk of islands where pepper grew, and of fish as long as a street.

“One more voyage,” said Sindbad to himself. “Only a short one.”

# --- page 2
So he bought bales of cloth and jars of sweet oil, and went down the river to Basra.

A ship lay at the quay with her long slanting sail tied up. Other merchants were going aboard, and Sindbad went with them.

A fair wind carried them down the Gulf and out upon the wide ocean. They sailed from port to port, wherever there was something to sell.

At every port Sindbad sold a little and bought a little. His bales grew smaller and his purse grew heavier.

“This is easy,” he said. “I should have come sooner.”

# --- page 3
One day they landed on an island of fruit trees and clear streams. Nobody lived there.

Sindbad ate his fill of fruit. Then he lay down by a stream, and the water sang him to sleep.

When he woke, the shore was empty. Far out on the water, the dhow was a white speck. Nobody had meant to leave him. Nobody had counted.

“Fool!” he shouted at himself. “You had a house! You had a fountain!”

He shouted until he was hoarse. Then, since shouting moved no ships, he climbed the tallest tree to look about.

# --- page 4
From the treetop he saw sea on one side. On the other, far off among the trees, something large and white was shining.

He walked to it. It was a dome, smooth as a washed bowl and higher than a house. He paced round it: fifty paces, and no door.

Then the sky went dark, as if a cloud had crossed the sun. It was a bird as wide as a cloud, and it settled on the dome.

Sindbad remembered the sailors’ talk of the Roc, the greatest bird in the world.

“That is no dome,” he whispered. “That is an egg.”

# --- page 5
The Roc folded her wings and slept upon her egg. One leg came down beside Sindbad, as thick as a palm trunk.

He looked at the leg. He looked at the empty sea.

“A bird this size does not live on one small island,” he said. “Where she goes, there will be land.”

He unwound his turban, yard after yard, and tied himself fast to the great leg. All night he did not dare to sneeze.

At dawn the Roc rose. Up went Sindbad, until the island was a leaf and the sea a blue plate. Then down she dropped, so fast that he could not breathe.

# Part II

# --- page 6
The Roc set him down in the deepest valley in the world.

He tore the knots loose and rolled away. The Roc snatched up a serpent longer than a ship, and was gone over the cliffs before he could stand.

The cliffs stood on every side like the walls of a well. No path climbed them. The sun reached the valley floor for one hour a day.

Sindbad sat down, and the ground crunched. He looked. He was sitting on diamonds.

They lay about like gravel in a stream bed, and some were as big as his fist.

# --- page 7
Sindbad laughed until the cliffs laughed back.

“On the island I had fruit and water, and wanted only a ship,” he said. “Here I have a king’s treasure, and I would give it all for a ladder.”

As the light failed, the serpents came out. By day they hid from the Roc in holes in the rock. Now they slid over the stones, each one as thick as a ship’s mast.

Sindbad found a small cave and rolled a stone across its mouth. He sat with his back to it all night and did not sleep. Outside, something dry went rustling by, and by, and by.

# --- page 8
At last a grey light showed round the stone. The rustling stopped. Sindbad crept out, stiff and hungry.

Thump! A great piece of raw meat landed beside him. Thump! Another fell farther off. He looked up, and saw only cliff and sky.

Then he remembered an old tale he had heard in Baghdad and never believed.

In it, merchants threw meat into a valley no man could climb down to. Diamonds stuck in the meat. Eagles carried the meat up to their nests, and the merchants chased the eagles off and picked out the stones.

“So it is true,” said Sindbad. “Then I know my way out.”

# --- page 9
He filled his bag and sash with the best stones, tied the biggest piece of meat to his back with his turban, and lay face down.

Claws closed on the meat. The valley fell away, and an eagle dropped him in a nest on the cliff top.

Men came shouting and banging sticks, and the eagle flew. A merchant ran to the nest and found a man where his meat should be.

“Do not be angry,” said Sindbad. “I have enough for us both.” He poured out his stones.

The merchant chose one, and only one. “This will keep me all my life,” he said.

# --- page 10
Sindbad went home the long way, with the merchants, stopping at one island after another. On one grew camphor trees so wide that a whole caravan could rest under a single one.

At last the ship came up the river to the date palms of Basra. From there a riverboat took him home to Baghdad.

He gave much of his treasure to the poor. He sat by his fountain and told his friends about the egg, the valley and the long night in the cave.

“I shall never go to sea again,” said Sindbad.

And he meant it, for a whole year.
