# Part I

# --- page 1
Androcles was a slave in a hot white town beside the sea.

He baked his master’s bread before dawn and carried his master’s water at noon. He owned nothing, not even his own two feet.

His master was a hard man with a hard voice. “Faster!” was the kindest word he ever said.

One night, when the house was asleep, Androcles lifted the bar of the gate. The hinge creaked. A dog turned over in its sleep.

Then he ran, past the last wall and the last olive tree, up into the dry hills where no road went.

# --- page 2
By noon the rocks were too hot to touch. His water skin was empty, and his tongue felt like a strip of leather.

Far below lay the yellow wheat fields and the blue sea. Rome’s empire ruled every shore of that sea, and its soldiers hunted runaway slaves on all of them.

“Then I must go where soldiers will not,” said Androcles, and he climbed higher.

Among the thorn bushes he found a cave. Inside, it was dim and cool, and the floor was soft sand. He lay down just to rest his eyes, and slept like a stone.

# --- page 3
A sound woke him: a low, rough groan, close by.

The mouth of the cave had gone dark. A lion stood in it, a lion with a black mane that hung to his knees. There was no other way out.

Androcles pressed his back to the rock and waited for the spring. It did not come.

The Lion limped in on three legs. Then he sat down and held out one great paw, the way a child shows a splinter.

The paw was swollen as round as a loaf. Deep in the pad stuck a thorn as long as a finger.

# --- page 4
“So that is your trouble,” whispered Androcles. “I have had thorns of my own.”

His hands shook. He crept forward and took the heavy paw into his lap. It burned against his knees.

He gripped the thorn with his finger and thumb. “Hold still, now. One, two…”

He pulled. Out came the thorn, and the Lion roared so that dust fell from the roof. Androcles shut his eyes.

Something warm and wet rasped across his hand, rough as a scrubbing brush. The Lion was licking him. Androcles laughed out loud, tore a strip from his tunic, and bound the paw up tight.

# --- page 5
So began the strangest household in all North Africa.

Each morning the Lion went hunting over the hills. Each evening he came back and laid the best of his catch at the man’s feet, like a guest bringing a gift.

Androcles had no fire. He laid his share on a flat rock and let the noon sun cook it.

At night the hills grew cold. Then he slept against the Lion’s side, which was as warm as an oven and rumbled with every breath.

“Nobody shouts ‘Faster!’ here,” Androcles told him. The Lion yawned. Three summers went by like that.

# Part II

# --- page 6
But a lion never answers, and Androcles grew hungry for voices.

One morning he scratched the Lion behind the ears and walked down toward the wheat fields. He only wanted to hear people talk about the weather.

He heard soldiers instead. They looked at his torn tunic and bare feet. “A runaway,” said their captain, and tied his wrists.

The law for runaways was cruel. Androcles was put on a grain ship and sent across the sea to Rome. There he must stand in the arena, the ring of sand where all Rome came to watch, and face a wild beast with empty hands.

# --- page 7
The noise reached him first. Rome was shouting, and it sounded like the sea in a storm.

A guard pushed him through a door into white sunlight. Sand stretched away on every side, raked as smooth as a tablecloth. Above the high wall sat more people than Androcles had seen in his whole life.

In a shaded box, under a purple cloth, sat the Emperor. He lifted one finger.

The shouting stopped. Across the sand a gate rattled up. Androcles looked at his hands. No stick, no stone, not so much as a thorn.

# --- page 8
Out came a lion. He was huge and thin, for his keepers had kept him hungry, and he crossed the sand in great leaps.

Androcles stood still. A ring has no corners to hide in.

Ten steps away, the lion stopped. His nose went up. He sniffed the air, once, twice. Then he made a low, rough sound that Androcles knew.

The great beast lay down in the sand and licked the man’s dusty feet. Androcles dropped to his knees and put both arms round the black mane.

“It is you!” he said. “They caught you too, old friend. How is the paw?”

# --- page 9
For one breath all Rome was silent. Then the crowd was on its feet, roaring louder than any lion.

The Emperor sent for Androcles. “That is the fiercest lion in Rome,” he said. “Why does he spare you?”

So Androcles told him about the cave, the thorn and the three summers. The story was written in big letters on a board and carried round the arena for all to read.

“Free the man! Free the lion!” shouted the people, row after row.

The Emperor looked at the lion leaning against the man’s legs. “Rome has spoken,” he said. “From today, nobody owns either of you.”

# --- page 10
After that, the two of them were seen all over Rome.

Androcles wore a clean tunic. The Lion walked beside him on a leash as thin as a ribbon, which he could have snapped with one yawn.

Bakers ran out with coins for the man. Children leaned from windows and threw flowers, until the black mane was pink with petals.

Wherever they went, people nudged each other and pointed. An old book still keeps what they said: “There goes the lion who was a man’s friend, and the man who was a lion’s doctor.”

The Lion paid no attention. He had smelled the butcher’s shop.
