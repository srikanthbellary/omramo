# How Wisdom Became the Property of the Human Race
# Omramo retelling, Medium tier. Manuscript for the text-fidelity check.
#
# This is our own retelling, written for Omramo, of a traditional Anansi story
# (Akan Anansesem, the spider stories of the Asante and Fante people of Ghana).
# It is not the wording of any earlier edition.
# Documented source for the tale: W. H. Barker and Cecilia Sinclair, "West African
# Folk-Tales" (London: George G. Harrap & Co., 1917), Project Gutenberg ebook #66923,
# https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66923

# --- page 1
Long ago, Father Anansi held all the wisdom in the world.

He lived in a village at the edge of a great green forest. Every day, his neighbours came knocking at his door.

“How do I mend my leaking roof?” asked one. “When should I plant my yams?” asked another.

Father Anansi always knew. He gave out his wisdom a little at a time, and he loved to be the one everybody needed.

# --- page 2
But one day, a neighbour forgot to say thank you. Another only laughed and said, “Oh, I knew that already!”

Father Anansi’s pride was stung. “Then they shall have no wisdom at all,” he grumbled. “I will take back every last crumb of it.”

All that day he went from house to house, gathering up every wise thing he had ever given away. He poured it all into a big clay pot, pressed on the lid, and sealed it tight.

# --- page 3
But Father Anansi’s young son, Kweku Tsin, was watching.

Kweku was a quiet boy who noticed things. Why was his father so busy? Why did he keep patting that pot and smiling to himself?

That evening, as the sky turned purple, Father Anansi tiptoed out of the house. His precious pot hung from a rope around his neck. Kweku Tsin slipped out after him, soft as a shadow.

# --- page 4
Father Anansi hurried down the forest path. He went on and on, until the houses of the village were far behind him.

The trees grew taller and the shadows grew deeper. At last he stopped beneath the tallest tree of all. Its trunk was wider than a doorway, and its top seemed to brush the moon.

“Up there,” he chuckled, “nobody will ever reach my wisdom—nobody but me.”

Behind a broad green leaf, Kweku held his breath and watched.

# --- page 5
Father Anansi hugged the trunk and began to climb.

But the pot hung in front of his tummy. He could not get his arms around the tree, and he could not see his feet. Up he went a little way—and the pot swung in his way.

Down he slid. He tried again. He puffed, he grunted, he stretched as far as he could—and the pot swung in his way.

Again and again he tried. Again and again, the pot swung in his way.

# --- page 6
Kweku Tsin watched from below. He bit his lip. He hopped from foot to foot. At last he could keep quiet no longer.

“Father!” he called. “Why not hang the pot on your back? Then it would not get in your way, and you could climb easily.”

Father Anansi stopped. He clung to the tree, quite still. Then, very slowly, he turned his head and looked down at his son.

# --- page 7
“All the wisdom in the world is meant to be inside this pot,” he said. “Yet my own small son knows a thing that I did not!”

His face grew hot. How foolish he felt! In a huff, he tugged the pot from his neck and flung it away.

Down it fell, down through the dark leaves. Crack! It landed on a big grey rock and burst into a hundred pieces.

# --- page 8
Out floated the wisdom, like sparks of gold.

The night wind carried it over the forest and over the village. It drifted across rivers and mountains and seas, to every land in the world.

A little of it settled on everyone—on farmers and fishers, on grandmothers and on babies.

Father Anansi watched it go. Then he gave a small smile, climbed down, took Kweku’s hand, and walked home.

And that is why no one has all the wisdom, and why everyone has some—even you.
