# Documented source for the tale: W. M. Flinders Petrie, “Egyptian Tales, Translated from the Papyri, First Series”
# (London: Methuen, 1895), Project Gutenberg ebook #7386, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/7386

# Left out: the frame story (the sailor reporting to his lord), the Serpent’s first threat, the animal sacrifices and the baboons and apes among the gifts.
# Softened: in the papyrus the shipmates are lost and the Serpent’s family is burned by a falling star. Here the crew comes back for the Sailor, and the Serpent says only that he is now alone.
# Numbers: Petrie gives a ship of 150 cubits and 150 sailors; later translations read 120 and 120, and we follow those. The Serpent’s question, four months, seventy-five serpents and two months home are from the tale.

# Part I

# --- page 1
Long ago in Egypt, a Sailor set out across the Red Sea.

His ship was the biggest on the water. A hundred and twenty sailors rowed it, each one braver than a lion.

They were going to the king’s mines, far, far away.

“The wind will be kind,” said the Captain. “We will be home before the figs are ripe.”

# --- page 2
Up went the great square sail. The wind filled it, round and white.

For days the ship ran over the blue water, and the sailors sang.

Then the sky turned dark. The wind began to howl. Waves stood up as tall as houses.

“Hold on!” shouted the Captain. “Hold on, my lions!”

# --- page 3
The ship went up, and the ship went down. Up, and down.

Then came the biggest wave of all. Crash! It swept the Sailor off the deck and into the sea.

He caught hold of a plank of wood. All night long the dark water carried him.

# --- page 4
In the morning a wave set him down on warm sand. He was on an island.

He looked for the great ship. He saw only water.

“I am all alone,” said the Sailor.

For three days he lay in the shade of a fig tree, too sad to move.

# --- page 5
Then he grew hungry. He stood up and looked around.

What an island! There were figs and grapes, fat melons, fish in the pools and birds in every tree.

He ate until he was full. Then he lit a little fire, to thank his gods.

That was when the thunder began.

# --- page 6
But it was not thunder. The trees shook. The ground shook. The Sailor shook most of all.

He hid his face in his hands. When he peeped out, a great Serpent was coming towards him.

His scales shone like gold. His eyebrows were blue as the bluest stone. He wore a long beard, like a king’s.

# --- page 7
The Serpent rose up above him.

“Who brought you here, little one?” he asked. “Who brought you here? Who brought you to my island?”

The Sailor lay flat on the sand. He opened his mouth, but no words came out.

So the Serpent picked him up in his mouth, as softly as a cat carries her kitten, and carried him away to his den.

# Part II

# --- page 8
The Serpent set him down without a scratch.

“Now, little one,” he said, “who brought you here?”

So the Sailor told him about the ship, the storm and the great wave. “I will never see my home again,” he said.

“Do not be afraid, little one,” said the Serpent. “In four months a ship will come from your own land and take you home.”

# --- page 9
So they waited, and counted the moons together.

One evening the Serpent told a story of his own. “Once there were seventy-five of us here: brothers, sisters, children. Now there is only me.”

The Sailor said nothing. He just sat a little closer.

“I was sad for a long time,” said the Serpent. “But I learned to be brave. So will you.”

# --- page 10
The Sailor wanted to give something back.

“When I get home,” he said, “I will send you ships full of incense and perfume!”

The Serpent laughed until his gold scales rattled.

“Little one, I am the lord of Punt. All the sweet smells in the world grow right here. Just say a good word for me in your town.”

# --- page 11
One moon passed, then two, then three, then four.

One morning the Sailor climbed a tall tree and looked out to sea. A sail!

It was a ship from Egypt, and he knew every face on board.

“There he is!” roared the Captain. “We have looked for you on every shore!”

# --- page 12
The Serpent heaped the ship with gifts: incense and perfume, sweet wood, and long white tusks of ivory.

The Sailor bowed low. “Thank you, my friend. I will come back to see you.”

“You cannot,” said the Serpent kindly. “When you sail away, my island will slip under the waves. No one will find it again. Go home, little one.”

# --- page 13
North they sailed for two months, just as the Serpent had said.

The Sailor looked back once. Where the island had been, there was only bright water.

At last the ship slid into the harbour of his own town. There were the palm trees. There were the flat brown roofs. And there, running along the bank, were his children.

# --- page 14
That night the whole town came to listen.

He told them about the storm, the wave and the island of figs.

Most of all, he told them about a great gold Serpent with blue eyebrows, who was kind to a frightened sailor.

Someone wrote it all down on papyrus. Four thousand years later, the good word has reached you.
