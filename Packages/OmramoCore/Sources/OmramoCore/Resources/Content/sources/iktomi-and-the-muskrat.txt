# Part I

# --- page 1
Iktomi sat by a prairie lake with a whole pot of fish.

All around him the grass of the Great Plains ran out to the edge of the sky. Wild rice stood thick in the shallows, and an old willow leaned over the water.

Iktomi was a trickster, who liked playing tricks far better than working. Meals did not come to him every day, but today he had more boiled fish than he could eat in two.

He stirred it with his horn spoon. He sniffed the steam. He hummed a dance song, and he did not look up once.

# --- page 2
“Good day, my friend!” said a voice from the wild rice.

Iktomi jumped so hard that he nearly swallowed his spoon. There at his elbow stood Muskrat, sleek and dripping, fresh from the lake.

“Good day,” said Iktomi, and that was all he said.

Now, among the Dakota, when a visitor finds you eating, you ask him to sit down and share the meal. “Come and sit, my friend. There is plenty for two.” Iktomi knew it as well as he knew his own name.

Muskrat smiled and waited to be asked. Iktomi hummed louder and tapped time on the rim of the pot.

# --- page 3
Muskrat looked at his wet toes. The lake behind him seemed a very good place to be.

Iktomi wanted every last fish for himself. He also wanted to look like the most generous fellow on the prairie.

He put down his spoon. “My friend, I have a fine idea!” he cried. “We shall run a race, from the far side of the lake back to this fire, and the winner eats the fish.”

“Race you?” said Muskrat. “You have long legs, and look at mine. They are no longer than your thumb.”

“Then I will run with a heavy rock tied on my back,” said Iktomi grandly. “Nobody can call that unfair.”

# --- page 4
They walked round to the far side of the lake, where grey stones lay in the shallows. Iktomi chose the biggest, to show how fair he was.

He spread out his blanket and rolled the stone into it. Muskrat pushed from below, Iktomi heaved from above, and up it went onto his back.

“Light as a duck feather,” said Iktomi, with his knees shaking.

Far across the water, a thin thread of smoke showed where the pot was waiting. The first to reach the fire would win.

“Ready?” asked Muskrat politely. “Go!” shouted Iktomi, and he was off.

# Part II

# --- page 5
The shore of the lake was much longer than it looked.

Iktomi ran through mud that sucked at his moccasins. He ran through reeds that slapped his face. The stone went thump, thump, thump against his back, and the mosquitoes sang in both his ears.

Halfway round he stopped to puff, and he looked across the water. Nothing moved on the other shore. No grass shook. No small brown runner hurried along it.

“Poor fellow,” said Iktomi. “He is so slow that I cannot even see him. I shall eat one fish for him.”

He laughed, hitched up his stone and ran on.

# --- page 6
But Muskrat was not on the other shore. Muskrat was not on any shore at all.

Nobody had said which way to go, so he had slipped into the lake without a splash. Now he swam straight across the middle, with only his nose and whiskers showing.

Out among the cattails stood his house, a round lodge of reeds heaped up in the water. He had dived and paddled here since he was a kit no bigger than a duck’s egg.

A long V of ripples spread out behind him, and the thread of smoke grew nearer with every stroke.

# --- page 7
The afternoon was nearly gone when Iktomi staggered up to the willow tree.

There lay the ashes, still warm. There lay his horn spoon. But there was no pot, no fish and no Muskrat.

Iktomi let go of the blanket. Down came the stone, thump, right on his toe.

He hopped round the ashes on one foot, howling. Then he sat down and held his toe in both hands.

“He swam!” he groaned. “Of course he swam. And I carried a rock round a lake! A pot shared is half a pot. A pot raced for is no pot at all.”

# --- page 8
All that running had made him thirsty. He limped to the lake and bent down to drink.

Then he stared. Down in the deep green water sat Muskrat, with the pot between his paws, picking a fish clean.

“So that is where you are hiding!” cried Iktomi. He plunged in his arm up to the shoulder and caught a handful of cold water.

Somebody laughed. The laugh came from over his head.

Iktomi looked up. On a branch of the old willow sat the real Muskrat with the real pot. The one in the lake was only his picture in the water.

# --- page 9
“Muskrat! Dear, kind Muskrat!” called Iktomi. “Throw me down a little. One fish. Even one bone would do!”

He tipped back his head and opened his mouth as wide as it would go.

Muskrat chose a bone and dropped it with care. It hit Iktomi on the nose, plink, and fell in the grass. Iktomi was on his knees at once, hunting for it.

Muskrat laughed until the branch shook. “Next time a friend comes while you are eating,” he said, “try saying this: ‘Come and sit, my friend. There is plenty for two.’ It is not hard.”

# --- page 10
Iktomi limped home across the prairie with an empty stomach and a toe as fat as a plum. Behind him, in the willow, Muskrat was scraping the pot.

All the way he practised. “Come and sit, my friend. There is plenty for two,” he told a meadowlark on a grass stem. “Come and sit, my friend,” he told a grasshopper, and a grey stone, and the first pale star.

By the time he reached home he had it perfectly. He lay down very pleased with himself.

And by morning, the storytellers say, he had forgotten every word.
