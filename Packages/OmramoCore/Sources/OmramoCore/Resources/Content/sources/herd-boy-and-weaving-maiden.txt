# This is our own retelling, written for Omramo, of the Chinese festival story of Qixi,
# the Herd Boy (Niulang) and the Weaving Maiden (Zhinu). It is not the wording of any earlier edition.
# Documented source for the tale: Richard Wilhelm (ed.), "The Chinese Fairy Book", translated by
# Frederick H. Martens (New York: Stokes, 1921), story XVI. Project Gutenberg ebook #29939,
# https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29939 (US public domain; other territories not verified).
# The source was read (pglaf mirror, 18 Nov 2026) for its plot only; no sentence follows its wording.
# VARIANT. Our telling departs from the cited book on purpose. In Wilhelm the boy takes the maiden's
# clothes while she bathes and so wins a promise of marriage; we do NOT tell that. We follow the equally
# old and widely told form: they meet at the river, marry, and each forgets their work; for that they are
# parted, and may meet one night a year if the work is done.
# Other departures from the cited book: it has a yellow cow, we have an old ox (the common form);
# it has nine sisters bathing in the Sea of Heaven, we have seven maidens washing silk in an earthly river;
# in it the maiden draws the river herself with her hairpin, here her grandmother the Queen Mother does
# (the common form, where she is Xiwangmu, the Queen Mother of the West); it has crows making the bridge,
# we have magpies, as in the Chinese tradition (the 'magpie bridge', queqiao).
# Plan change: the plan named both the Lord of Heaven and the Queen Mother. We keep one ruler, the
# Queen Mother, as Zhinu's grandmother, so a child follows one authority from parting to pardon.
# Our own small inventions: the dropped skein that Niulang fetches, the spider's web on the idle loom,
# the ox as wedding guest, the magpies carrying the news. The grey dawns are in many tellings.
# Left out: the stolen clothes, the talking willow, the couple's two children, the death of the ox and
# its hide (in common tellings Niulang flies up wearing the hide; here the living ox carries him),
# the yoke and spindle the pair throw at each other, and the promise of immortality.
# Softened: no one is punished in anger; the Queen Mother frowns, parts them, and relents.
# From the tradition and kept: rain on the seventh night as the lovers' tears (also in Wilhelm);
# magpies bald after Qixi from being walked on (a common saying, not in Wilhelm).
# Reviewer to check: the names Zhinu (with u-umlaut in the text) and Niulang and the pronunciation hints

# Part I

# --- page 1
High above the clouds, a girl sat weaving the colours of the sky.

Her name was Zhinü, which means the Weaving Maiden. Her grandmother, the Queen Mother, ruled the whole country of the sky.

On a loom as tall as a pine tree, Zhinü wove the silk of the sky. She wove rose for the dawn and gold for the evening, and every day the sky wore what she had made.

It was fine work, and it was never finished. As soon as one evening was done, the next dawn was waiting. Zhinü had never had a day of rest.

# --- page 2
Far below, beside a slow brown river, lived a boy called Niulang. The name means the Herd Boy, and it suited him.

He had no mother or father. He had a hut with a straw roof, a row of mulberry trees, and one old yellow ox.

Niulang shared his supper with the ox. Each evening he combed the burrs out of the old beast’s coat and told him about the day.

One night, under a sky full of stars, the Old Ox answered.

“Tomorrow at dusk the sky maidens come down to wash their silk in the river,” he said. “Go and see.”

# --- page 3
The next evening Niulang led the ox down to the river to drink. Down the sky came seven maidens, their arms full of silk.

Six washed their silk and flew home. The seventh was rinsing a skein of dawn-pink thread when it slipped from her fingers. Niulang waded in and caught it.

“Thank you,” said Zhinü. “What is it like to live down here?”

He told her about ploughing, and rain on the roof, and the smell of cut grass. They talked until the stars came out.

She came back the next evening, and the next. Then one evening she did not go home.

# --- page 4
Zhinü and Niulang were married under the mulberry trees, with the Old Ox for a guest.

He ploughed and she wove plain brown cloth, and at night they sat in the doorway and counted stars. They were so happy that they forgot everything else.

Some mornings the Old Ox stood unfed until noon. Weeds crept across the field.

“The field is waiting,” said the Old Ox. “Tomorrow,” said Niulang.

And far above them the great loom stood still. Dawn came up grey. Evening went down grey. All over the world, people looked up and wondered where the colours had gone.

# --- page 5
In the sky country, the Queen Mother looked out at another grey evening and frowned.

She went to the weaving hall. Dust lay on the tall loom, and a spider had spun its own small web across the threads.

“Where is my granddaughter?” she asked. No one dared to answer, so she looked down through the clouds herself. There was Zhinü, laughing in a doorway, with weeds all round the house.

“The sky cannot go without its colours,” said the Queen Mother. “Bring her home.”

Two messengers in robes of white cloud bowed, and stepped off the edge of the sky.

# Part II

# --- page 6
The messengers came at dusk, as quietly as mist.

They took Zhinü by the hands, and her feet left the ground. “Niulang!” she called. Already she was as high as the mulberry tops.

Niulang ran. He ran until the field ended and there was nothing left to run on but air.

Behind him the Old Ox knelt in the grass. “You fed me when you had little,” he said. “Climb on my back and hold my horns.”

Then the old beast leaped. His hooves struck the evening air like a road, and up they went, past the clouds, into the dark.

# --- page 7
The stars grew big around them. Ahead flew the messengers, and between them Zhinü, looking back.

“Faster, old friend!” cried Niulang. The gap closed. Zhinü stretched out her hand, and his fingers brushed hers.

But the Queen Mother stood waiting at the gate of the sky. She drew a long silver pin from her hair and scored one line across the dark.

The line shone. Then it filled with stars, thousands and thousands, and flooded into a river too wide to swim and too deep to wade.

People in China still call it the Silver River.

# --- page 8
Niulang sat down on one bank and would not leave. Zhinü sat on the other and would not weave.

After many grey days, Zhinü stood up and went back to her loom.

“If the dawn must have colour,” she said, “it shall be the best I ever made.” Across the water, Niulang began to herd the cattle of the sky, and not one went hungry.

The magpies saw it all, and chattered about it in every tree. At last the Queen Mother heard, and her frown softened.

“One night a year,” she said. “The seventh night of the seventh month. If the work is done.”

# --- page 9
All year Zhinü wove and Niulang herded, and the dawns were finer than ever before.

When the seventh night came, a sound rose from the earth like wind in dry leaves. It was wings.

Magpies were flying up from every wood and rooftop in the world. They locked wing to wing, thousands upon thousands, until a black and white bridge arched from bank to bank.

Zhinü stepped on from one side, and Niulang from the other. The bridge trembled under them, feather-soft.

They met at the very top, and for one whole night nobody in the sky did any work at all.

# --- page 10
They are up there still. On a clear summer night, look for the pale river of light overhead.

On one bank shines a bright star, and on the other bank another. People in China say those two stars are the Weaving Maiden and the Herd Boy, each hard at work.

If rain falls on the seventh night, people say it is their tears. Nobody can tell whether they are sad ones or happy ones.

And the next morning, look closely at the magpies. Some seem a little bald on top. So would you be, if two people in love had walked across your head.
