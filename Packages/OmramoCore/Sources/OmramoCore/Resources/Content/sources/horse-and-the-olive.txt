# Part I

# --- page 1
The ancient Greeks told of a town that had no name.

It began as a few huts on a high rock near the sea. The rock was steep on every side and flat on top, like a table set down on the dry plain.

King Cecrops and his people had built the huts themselves, of mud brick and thatch. They had piled up a wall of stones. They had a spring of cold water and forty goats.

“We have everything a town needs,” said Cecrops, “except something to call it.”

# --- page 2
It was not an easy place. The soil was thin and full of stones. All summer long the sun baked the hillsides, and hardly a drop of rain fell.

Still, the people were proud of it. Every stone in that wall had been carried up in somebody’s arms.

Far above, two of the gods were watching. One was Poseidon, lord of the sea, whose voice was like waves in a cave. The other was Athena, the grey-eyed goddess of wisdom and of clever hands.

Each of them looked at the brave little town on its rock, and each of them wanted it.

# --- page 3
“It stands beside my sea,” boomed Poseidon. “It is mine!”

“Its people build with their hands and think before they speak,” said Athena. “It is mine.”

They argued until the clouds shook. At last Zeus, the king of the gods, grew tired of the noise.

“Enough,” he said. “Go down to the rock, both of you, and each give the town one gift. Its people shall choose, and whoever gives the better gift shall name it.”

So the two of them came down onto the hilltop, taller than the huts and shining like bronze. The goats ran. The people stood very still.

# --- page 4
Poseidon went first. He strode to the middle of the rock and lifted his great three-pronged spear.

Crack! The hill shook. The stone split open, salt spray burst out of it, and through the spray leaped an animal no one there had ever seen.

It was a horse, white as sea foam. He tossed his mane and drummed his hooves on the rock, and the children hid behind their mothers.

“No runner will ever catch you again!” roared Poseidon. “Harness him to a chariot, and your enemies will scatter like sparrows!”

The people cheered until their throats were sore.

# Part II

# --- page 5
Then it was Athena’s turn, and she did not shout.

She pushed her helmet back from her face and knelt on the bare rock. She pressed the end of her spear into a crack in the stone, as gently as a gardener.

A green shoot curled out of the crack. It grew to a thin grey trunk, no taller than a man. It put out narrow leaves, silver underneath, and then a scatter of small hard fruit.

Nobody cheered. The wind turned the little leaves over, green, silver, green.

“Is that all?” said somebody at the back, and somebody else laughed.

# --- page 6
Athena did not mind the laughing. She picked one of the small fruits and held it out on her palm.

“This is an olive,” she said. “Soak it in sea water and it is good to eat. Press it, and it gives golden oil: oil for your bread, oil for your lamps, oil to rub on tired skin and to soothe the sick.”

“The tree asks for no rich soil and very little rain. It will grow on your stones. And it will still be giving fruit to your grandchildren’s grandchildren.”

# --- page 7
Then she broke off one leafy twig. “And when you carry a branch of it in your hand,” she said, “strangers will know that you come in peace.”

The people looked from the tree to the horse and back again.

“But the horse!” cried a young man. “No enemy would ever dare to come near us!”

An old woman shook her head. She had known hungry winters. “Can we eat a gallop?” she asked. “Can we light a lamp with it?”

A small girl said nothing at all. She held one olive up to the sun and turned it round and round.

# --- page 8
King Cecrops set two clay jars on the ground, one for the horse and one for the olive. Every man and woman dropped a pebble into the jar they chose. Clink. Clink. Clink.

Then he tipped them out and counted, twice, to be sure.

“The olive has it,” he said, “by one pebble.”

Poseidon’s beard bristled like a wave about to break. He stamped back down to the sea, and for a whole week the waves banged on the shore like fists.

But he did not take back his horse.

# --- page 9
The people kept the white horse and were glad of him. He carried riders and news along the dusty roads, far more often than he ever went to war.

As for the town, it had a name at last. They called it Athens, after Athena.

The people cut twigs from her tree and planted them, and their children planted more. Before long, olive trees silvered every hillside around the rock.

The oil went down to the harbour in tall clay jars, and ships carried it to every port on the sea. Athens grew rich, one jar at a time.

# --- page 10
Long afterwards, the Athenians built Athena a marble temple on top of that same rock. High on one end they carved the contest in stone: the goddess and the sea god, face to face.

And beside their temples they always kept one olive tree growing, for they said it was the very tree she planted.

Kings have come and gone since then. The marble has cracked, and most of the carved gods have fallen.

But climb the rock today and you will find an olive tree there, planted in memory of the first one. The wind still turns its leaves over, green, silver, green.
