# Part I

# --- page 1
Elisapie rode across the white land of Nunavut on a sled.

The sled was a long wooden qamutiik. It bumped and hissed over the snow behind Grandfather’s snowmobile.

It was a spring evening. The sun sat low and gold, and it would not go down for hours.

“Faster, Grandfather!” shouted Elisapie. “The fish are waiting!”

# --- page 2
Grandfather did not go faster. Grandfather never went faster.

They crossed the frozen sea. They crossed a hill of bare brown rock. They crossed more snow than Elisapie could look at.

At last he stopped beside a frozen lake. “The fishing lakes are far,” he said. “Tonight we sleep here.”

Elisapie looked around. No house. No cabin. Only snow.

# --- page 3
“Sleep in what?” she asked.

“In a house,” said Grandfather. He patted the snow. “We have not built it yet.”

He meant an iglu. In Inuktitut, the language of Inuit, iglu means a house. This one would be made of snow.

“I will help! I will be fast!” said Elisapie. Grandfather said only, “Hm,” and took out his snow knife.

# --- page 4
Elisapie did not wait. She took the saw and cut a block from the snow at her feet.

“One!” She heaved it up. Crack! It broke in two.

“Two!” The next block crumbled like cake. “Three!” The third fell to powder.

Grandfather looked at the heap. “A fine pile of snow,” he said. “It is not a house.”

# --- page 5
“Why do they break?” asked Elisapie.

Grandfather did not say. He walked off slowly, pushing his knife into the snow. Too soft here. Too soft there.

Then the knife went in slow and even, all the way down. Squeak!

“This snow sings,” he said. “The wind packed it hard. Soft snow is for jumping in. Singing snow is for houses.”

# --- page 6
He cut. Down, down, across, and a push with his boot. Out came a block as long as his arm, straight as a box.

He gave Elisapie the saw. “Now you. Slowly.”

Down, down, across. Her tongue stuck out. Her block was smaller, but it came out whole. “It is heavy!” she puffed.

“Good,” said Grandfather. “A house should be.”

# --- page 7
Block after block they cut. Grandfather stood them in a ring on the snow, each tight against the next.

Then he did something strange. He sliced the top of the ring into a slope, low at one end and high at the other.

“You have spoiled it!” cried Elisapie. “It is all crooked!”

“Crooked on purpose,” said Grandfather. “Watch.”

# Part II

# --- page 8
Grandfather set the next block on the low end of the slope.

He set another beside it, leaning on the first. Then another, leaning on that. The wall did not go up in rings. It climbed round and round, like a snail’s shell.

“Each block leans on the last,” said Grandfather. “So none of them can fall.”

# --- page 9
Grandfather worked inside the wall, cutting blocks from the snow under his boots.

Elisapie had the outside. “Find every gap,” he said, “and fill it with soft snow. Your kind of snow.”

She pressed snow into the cracks with her mitts. Pat, pat, pat.

Each new block tipped inward a little more. Nothing fell. Each block leaned on the last.

# --- page 10
Up went the wall, and in. Soon Elisapie could see only Grandfather’s hood. Then only his knife. Then only a hole with a voice in it.

“Are you still in there?” she called.

“I hope so,” said the voice. “Somebody is building this house.”

The wall had become a dome, round as a bowl turned upside down.

# --- page 11
He cut the last block too big. He pushed it up through the hole sideways and laid it flat.

Shave, shave went his knife. The block sank down, snug as a lid.

“Grandfather! How will you get out?”

The knife poked through the wall by her boots and sawed a small door. Grandfather looked out. “Same way you get in.”

# --- page 12
Inside, Grandfather unwrapped Grandmother’s stone lamp, the qulliq. He poured in oil and lit the wick.

A low flame crept along the edge of the lamp. The white walls turned gold.

Elisapie looked up at the blocks, winding round to the top. “Which ones are mine?”

“The ones holding up mine,” said Grandfather. “And mine are holding up yours.”

# --- page 13
They ate bannock and drank hot, sweet tea. The wind went round and round the house and found no way in.

Outside, the spring night stayed pale and blue. The little house glowed like a lamp.

Elisapie lay warm in her sleeping bag and counted the blocks. One, two, three…

She was asleep before she reached the top.

# --- page 14
In the morning she crawled out into bright sun. There stood the snow house, round and firm. Not one block had moved.

“Hurry, Grandfather! The fish are waiting!”

Grandfather sat in the doorway with his tea. “They have waited all winter,” he said. “They can wait for one cup.”

So Elisapie sat down and waited too. Slowly. Well, nearly slowly.
