# Part I

# --- page 1
King Khufu was building the biggest tomb in the world.

It was a stone pyramid on the rock at Giza, and only half done. Day after day the king’s great tomb swallowed stone, and still it wanted more.

The king could only wait, and waiting made him cross. Each afternoon he sat in his cool hall of columns and called for his sons.

“Tell me a tale of wonders,” he said.

One son told of a magician who folded a lake in half like a blanket. Another told of a wax crocodile that came alive. The king yawned. Those magicians had all died long ago.

# --- page 2
Then Prince Hordedef stood up. He was the quiet son, the one who was always reading.

“Those are old tales, Father,” he said. “Nobody alive saw them happen. I know of a magician who is living today.”

The king stopped yawning.

“His name is Dedi, and he is a hundred and ten years old. Every day he eats five hundred loaves and drinks a hundred jugs of beer. A lion follows him about like a dog, and nobody needs to hold its lead. People say that if a head is cut off, he can put it back.”

“Five hundred loaves?” said the king.

# --- page 3
“There is one thing more,” said the prince. “Dedi knows about the secret rooms of Thoth.”

Now the king sat up straight. The Egyptians said that Thoth was the god of wisdom and of writing, and that his temple held secret rooms no one could find. Khufu had hunted for their plan for years. He wanted rooms just like them inside his own tomb.

“Go yourself, my son,” said the king. “Take my best boat up the Nile and bring this Dedi to me.”

Before the sun went down, the sailors were loading the boats.

# --- page 4
The prince’s river boat went south with the wind in its big square sail. Mud-brick villages and palm trees slid by.

He found Dedi at his door, lying in the shade on a bed of palm ribs, while two servants rubbed the aches out of his head and his feet.

The prince bowed low. “May your old age be long and free of aches. My father the king invites you to his table.”

“Welcome, king’s son,” said Dedi, holding out his hands to be pulled up. “I will come. But my books come too.”

So Dedi sailed in the prince’s boat, and his books sailed behind.

# Part II

# --- page 5
Dedi walked into the hall of columns leaning on his staff.

He was as small as a boy and as wrinkled as a dried fig. Behind him came two servants with a chest of papyrus rolls.

“So you are Dedi. Why have you never come to my court?” asked King Khufu.

“Nobody ever sent for me, my lord,” said Dedi. “Today somebody did, and here I am.”

The king leaned forward. “Is it true that you can put back a head that has been cut off?” “I know how it is done, my lord,” said Dedi.

# --- page 6
“Then we shall see it,” said the king. He clapped his hands. “Fetch a prisoner from the prison.”

The guards turned to go. Nobody ever said no to Khufu.

“Not a person, my lord king,” said Dedi quietly. “A herdsman would not do that to his oxen. It is not right.”

The fan-bearers forgot to fan. The king could have ordered it with one word. He looked a long time at the small old man, and the small old man looked back.

“Bring a goose,” said King Khufu.

# --- page 7
The goose came in under a servant’s arm, hissing at everybody.

It was done behind a cloth, quick as a blink. Then the goose’s body lay at the west side of the hall, and its head lay at the east side, looking most offended.

Dedi lifted his staff and spoke words that no one else knew.

The body got up and waddled. The head went hop, hop, hop across the floor. They met in the middle, and there stood one whole goose, cackling crossly at the king.

The court gasped, and then it laughed.

# --- page 8
When the hall was quiet again, the king asked what he had wanted to ask all along.

“Now, Dedi. The secret rooms of Thoth. How many are there?”

“Forgive me, my lord. I do not know the number,” said Dedi. “I only know where the answer is kept. It lies in a chest of flint, in a temple in the City of the Sun.”

“Then bring the chest to me.”

Dedi shook his white head. “It is not mine to bring, my lord. Not even for a king.”

# --- page 9
Twice in one afternoon the old man had said no.

The princes stared at the floor. The goose, for once, kept quiet. King Khufu frowned until his eyebrows met. Then, slowly, he began to laugh.

“I have a hundred men who say yes to everything,” he said. “I had better keep the one who says no.”

He gave his orders. Dedi was to live in Prince Hordedef’s house. Every day he was to have an ox, a thousand loaves, a hundred jugs of beer and a hundred bunches of onions.

“The onions are a kind thought,” said Dedi.

# --- page 10
Late that afternoon the king and the magician stood on the palace terrace.

Beyond the green fields, the half-built pyramid caught the last light. Tiny men hauled a stone up its long ramp, singing as they pulled.

“Year after year of stone, and still no top,” said Khufu. “Could your words finish it by morning, Dedi?”

“Some things are better made by many hands, my lord,” said Dedi. “Besides, I have a thousand loaves to eat.”

The king laughed. Down in the courtyard a goose was still complaining to anyone who would listen. And far off, one more stone slid into its place.
