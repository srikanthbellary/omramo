# The White-Haired Child

# --- page 1
This tale comes from the Book of Kings, and it begins with a man who longed for a son.

His name was Sam, son of Nariman, and in all of Iran there was no warrior like him. When he rode out, the enemy’s horses smelled him on the wind and would not go forward. His great mace hung by his saddle, and his name alone could end a quarrel.

He had palaces in Zabulistan, gardens where water ran in tiled channels between the cypresses, and more gold than he could count. What he did not have was a child. Year after year he stood at the window in the early light and watched the garden paths, as if a son might come walking up them.

At last his wife told him that a child was coming. Sam, who had faced armies without a tremor, found that his hands shook. He went out into the garden and walked the paths until the stars went pale.

# --- page 2
The baby came at last. He was strong and long-limbed, with a firm grip and a loud voice, and his eyes were dark and bright. But his hair was white. Not pale, not fair: white as the snow on the far peaks, like the hair of a man of ninety.

For a week no one dared to tell Sam. This was the man who guarded the kings of Persia, and who could say a hard word to him? The women hushed each other in the corridors. The baby’s mother held him close, named him Zal, and told no one that she was afraid.

In the end it was the old nurse who went. She found Sam on the terrace above the pool and bowed low, and then, because there was no gentle way to say it, she said it plainly.

“My lord, you have a son. He is whole and well, and he grips a finger like a blacksmith. Only his hair is white, white as an old man’s. Apart from that, he is perfect. Will you come and see?”

# --- page 3
Sam came. He stood over the cradle a long time, and the child looked up at him with dark, steady eyes under that cap of snowy hair.

Then Sam thought of his warriors, and of the lords at the king’s table, and of how they would lean their heads together. “They will say this is a child of ill luck,” he said. “They will say it is a sign against my house. They will laugh at me behind their hands.”

His counsellors agreed with him, as counsellors do. Only the mother begged. She knelt and held his sleeve and asked him to look again, only look. Sam would not look again. He gave the order that the child be carried to the mountains, far from any road, and left there.

A rider took the bundle out through the garden gate before the sun was up. Sam did not watch him go. But that night, and for many nights after, the greatest warrior in Iran lay awake and listened to the fountains, and could not sleep.

# --- page 4
North of the plains the Alborz mountains stand like a wall, ridge behind ridge, and above them all floats the white cone of Damavand. The rider climbed until his horse could climb no more. Then he laid the child on a flat rock in the sun, tucked the cloak around him, and turned back without a word.

The mountain was very quiet. Wind combed the dry grass. A lizard came out onto the warm stone, looked at the bundle, and went away again. The child slept, and woke, and found the world empty, and began to cry.

High above, on a crag no climber had ever reached, the Simurgh lifted her head. She was the oldest of all birds. When she spread her wings, the story says, their shadow could cover a valley, and her eyes had watched the mountains since before there were kings.

She had chicks of her own in the nest behind her, and she knew every sound these slopes could make. This one was new: thin and furious, rising on the wind. She opened her great wings and slid down the air towards it.

# --- page 5
She came down beside the rock with a sound like a sail filling, and folded her wings, and looked. The child had stopped crying. He lay with both small hands spread open, staring up at her, at the curved beak and the crest and the long tail spilling colours over the stones.

She saw the fine cloak, and the white hair, and the empty slope where a horse’s hoofprints led away downhill. She understood more than she wished to. A man had done this. A father, very likely, with a house full of servants and no room in it for this.

The story says that pity moved in her then, the way warmth moves into stone at sunrise. She bent her head and breathed on him. Then, as carefully as if he were an egg, she closed her claws around the bundle.

She climbed the air in wide, unhurried turns, the slope shrinking below, the rock and the hoofprints and the whole wide land of men falling away. The child did not cry. Above the last snow, above the clouds, her nest was waiting.

# The Nest

# --- page 6
The nest sat on the very top of the crag, where only wind and birds ever came.

It was built of ebony and sandalwood, branches carried from forests a week’s ride away, and it smelled warm and sweet in the sun. Her chicks, already the size of goats, peered over the rim. They stared at the newcomer, and he stared at them, and nobody cried.

The Simurgh fed him as she fed her own, though she had to learn what a human child could eat: soft things, warm things, given little and often. At night she drew him under her wing, where the wind could not reach, and he slept against feathers longer than his whole body.

She could not call him by the name his mother had given him, for she had never heard it. She gave him a name of her own: Dastan. The chicks learned to say it before he did.

# --- page 7
Years went by on the mountain, and the boy grew. He grew tall and long-armed, quick on the rocks as a wild goat, and his hair grew long and stayed as white as the day he was born. In the nest it was nothing strange. The Simurgh’s own crest was white at the tips.

She taught him what she knew, which was a great deal. She taught him to read the weather in the colour of the morning, and the names of the stars that wheel over the peaks, and the talk of birds, which is mostly about wind. On clear days she carried him on her back over the whole of the land, so that he saw rivers like threads and cities like spilled salt.

“Who lives in those?” Zal asked her once, when they passed over a city of roofs and gardens.

“Your own kind,” said the Simurgh. “They build walls to keep the wind out, and then they wonder why they cannot hear it.” She banked, and the city slid away under her wing, and he watched it until it was gone.

# --- page 8
Below the mountain ran one of the old roads, and along it went the caravans: long strings of camels with bells at their necks, carrying silk and salt and copper from one end of the empire to the other.

The drivers began to tell a strange thing. High on the crag, they said, where nobody could climb, a young man stood at the edge of a giant nest. He was taller than any of them. His hair blew out behind him like a white flag. And when the great bird came home, he ran to meet her.

Some called it a lie, and some a wonder, and some a warning. But a story that is told at every well and every inn will not stay small. It went from the caravan drivers to the market traders, and from the traders to the soldiers, and from the soldiers, at last, to the house of Sam.

Sam heard it at supper, from a guest who meant it as an idle tale. He set his cup down very slowly. He asked one question, then another. Then he said nothing at all, and the guest wondered what he had done wrong.

# --- page 9
Sam had not slept well in all those years. His beard, which had been black when he sent the rider out, had gone grey and then white. He did not like to look at it in the water of the pool.

That night he dreamed. A young rider came towards him across a plain, with a banner streaming and horsemen behind him, and at the rider’s side walked a wise man in a white robe. The wise man stopped in front of Sam and looked at him without any kindness at all.

“You threw away a son for the colour of his hair,” he said. “Look at your own beard. Is it any darker? A bird has raised your child, a wild thing of the mountains, and done what you would not. What kind of father is that?”

Sam woke in the grey hour with his face wet. In the morning he called his wise men and told them the dream, and they did not soften it. “It is true,” they said. “Go and find him. Go now.”

# --- page 10
He rode north with his horsemen behind him, the way the rider had gone so long ago, and the mountains rose up blue and then grey and then white. At the foot of the crag he left his horse and began to climb.

It was no use. The rock went up sheer and smooth, polished by a thousand winters, and his hands found nothing to hold. He tried one face and then another. He was an old man in a coat of mail, and the crag did not care who he was.

So he stood at the bottom with his men silent behind him, and lifted his white head. “I was ashamed of the wrong thing,” he cried. “I was afraid of what people would say, and I let it make me cruel. If my son is alive up there, let him forgive me.”

“Forgive me. Come down, my son.”

His voice went up the rock face and broke against the wind. For a long moment nothing answered. Then, high above, at the very top of the crag, something moved: a great head with a crest, turning to listen.

# The Return

# --- page 11
The Simurgh listened until the voice below had stopped, and then she turned to Zal.

“That is your father,” she said. “He is Sam, son of Nariman, the greatest warrior in Iran, and he has come to take you home. You must go down to him. There is a whole life waiting for you in the world of people, Dastan, and it is larger than this nest.”

Zal looked over the rim, at the small grey figure on the rock far below, and then back at her. He had never once thought of leaving. “Have I been a burden to you?” he asked. “Is that why? Have I grown too big, or eaten too much, or been too slow to learn? Tell me what I did, and I will mend it.”

The great bird bent her head until her eye was level with his. It was an old eye, gold at the rim, and it had seen kings come and go. “You were the best thing this mountain ever held,” she said. “That is why I will not hold you.”

# --- page 12
Then she reached back with her beak and drew one long feather from her breast, striped turquoise and saffron and rose, and laid it in his hands.

“Keep this against your heart,” she said. “If ever you are in danger, or in trouble you cannot see a way through, put it to a flame. Wherever I am, I will know, and I will come.”

Zal could not speak. He pressed his face against her neck feathers, the way he had when he was small, and breathed in sandalwood and wind.

The story says he kept it all his life, and that years later, on the night his own son Rostam was born, he burned it. The danger was great that night, and the wings came down out of the dark as she had promised. But that is another tale.

Then she spread her wings, and he climbed onto her back as he had a thousand times. The nest, the chicks, the only home he had ever known, all dropped away behind them as she glided down towards the waiting men.

# --- page 13
She came down onto the flat rock at the foot of the crag, and the horses shied and the soldiers stepped back. Zal slid from her back. For the first time since he was a baby, he stood among people.

Sam looked at him: at the height of him, the long white hair, the face that was his own face made young. Then the greatest warrior in Iran went down on his knees on the stone, and wept, and did not hide it.

“I left you on a mountain because I was afraid of a few words,” he said. “A bird had more heart than I did. I cannot give you back those years. I can only ask you to forgive me.”

Zal looked at him for a long time. The soldiers waited. The wind moved in the grass. Then Zal stepped forward and took his father’s hands, and raised him up.

“I forgive you,” he said. And Sam, holding his son’s hands, spoke the name the boy’s mother had given him all those years ago, a name Sam had never once said aloud. “Zal,” he said. “Zal. Come home.”

# --- page 14
Sam bowed low to the Simurgh, as he had never bowed to any king, and thanked her. She lowered her great head to him once. Then she lifted into the air, and the wind of her wings flattened the grass for a hundred paces around.

They rode south together, father and son, at the head of the long column. Zal had never sat on a horse. He held on badly and laughed at himself, and Sam laughed with him, and the soldiers behind them looked at each other and began, slowly, to smile.

At the last pass he turned in the saddle. The mountain stood behind them, white against the morning, and far above it a great bird was circling, very small now, and very slow. He put his hand to his chest, where the feather lay against his heart, and kept it there until the road turned and she was gone.

In Iran, storytellers still tell this tale in the tea-houses, pacing and turning with a stick in hand. When they reach the moment the great wings come down to the old man on the rock, the whole room goes quiet, and listens.
