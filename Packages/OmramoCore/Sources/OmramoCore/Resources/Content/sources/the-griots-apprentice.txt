# The Apprentice

# --- page 1
Every morning before the first cock crowed, Sira’s grandfather made her say the names.

Sira sat on the cold step of his doorway with her knees under her chin, and the names came out of the dark one after another. A father, his father, his father’s father, back seven generations, for each family her grandfather sang for. Who had married whom. Who had crossed which river. Who had dug the first well in which town.

If she missed one, he did not scold her. He only said “Again,” from his mat inside the door, and she began that family from the top.

Sira was born into a family of jeliw, and a jeli keeps what no one writes down. In the great towns, scholars wrote in Arabic, but the deeds of families lived in voices. Lose a name, her grandfather said, and you lose a person twice.

By the time the sky above the wall turned the colour of milk, she had reached the family of Bakary the merchant, and she slowed down. That family she said with special care. That family paid for the roof over their heads and the millet in their pot.

# --- page 2
When the light came, her cousin Lamine carried out the balafon and set it under the baobab. Its wooden keys sat on a frame of bent sticks, and beneath each key hung a gourd, small ones for the high notes, big as a water pot for the low. Lamine was fourteen and played better than he talked.

Sira did not play. In their family the men played and the women sang, and her grandfather said her voice could carry across a market without shouting. He taught her the way he had been taught, line by line. He sang; she sang it back. Nothing was written, so nothing could be lost, except by forgetting.

Strangers would one day call people like them griots. Sira had never heard the word. She knew only what her grandfather told her: “A family without its jeli is a family without a past. And a family without a past has nothing to stand on.”

Beyond the wall, the River Niger was rising, as it rose every year after the rains, spreading brown and slow over the fields. Fishermen poled past with their nets heaped in the bottoms of their boats. Sira sang the names to the river, and the river did not argue.

# --- page 3
Late in the rains, a boat with a striped awning tied up below the compound, and Bakary the merchant climbed the bank with four servants behind him. He was a broad man in a robe so wide it moved like a sail, with a ring of gold on his thumb.

The servants set down their gifts: a sack of millet, a bolt of indigo cloth, a basket of red kola nuts. Last came a small bundle of white cotton, tied with string, that one servant carried in both hands as though it were heavier than it looked.

The two men went inside, and Sira was sent to fetch water. She took a long time over it. When she crept back past the doorway, she heard only the end. “Sing all of it, old friend. The founding, the journeys, the wells. But not the salt road.”

Then there was a silence so long that a lizard ran the whole length of the wall. At last she heard the bundle being untied, and the dry whisper of gold dust pouring into her grandfather’s brass dish. Her grandfather said nothing to stop it.

# --- page 4
A week later, her grandfather’s voice broke in the middle of a song. He coughed, tried again, and nothing came but a dry rasp, like a reed split down its length. By evening he lay on his mat with a fever.

“Bakary’s grandson is to be named in Djenné in nine days,” he whispered. “I promised to sing. You will sing instead. Lamine will play. The gold is paid.” Then, with Lamine at the balafon picking out the old tune, he taught her the history of Bakary’s family, line by line, the whole of it. And at the end he taught her the part that was not to be sung.

Long ago, Bakary’s grandfather Dramane had crossed the desert with a salt trader named Yaya. A sandstorm buried their camp. Yaya dug Dramane out with his bare hands and carried him to a well. They came home and sold the salt for gold, and Dramane kept all of it. Yaya’s family had been poor ever since.

“You must know all of it,” he said, when she had sung it back to him without a mistake. “A jeli who knows only the sweet parts is a parrot. Know all of it. Then sing what was paid for.”

# --- page 5
That night Sira could not sleep. She lay on her mat and listened to the frogs in the flooded fields, and to her grandfather’s breath scraping in and out in the next room. The history went round and round in her head, and every time it came to the salt road, it stopped, like a foot finding a missing step in the dark.

She thought of Yaya digging in the sand with his bare hands. She thought of the gold dust whispering into the brass dish. She thought of seventy years of a family eating less than they should, so that another family could eat more.

At last she got up and went to his doorway. The lamp had burned down to a blue bead. “Grandfather,” she said. “You always say a jeli’s word is worth more than gold. Why did you take it?”

For a long time he did not answer. Outside, a dog barked once and was quiet. Then the old man turned on his mat, slowly, as if it hurt him, and lay with his face to the wall.

# The Feast

# --- page 6
Two days later, Sira and Lamine went down to the river with the balafon wrapped in a cloth.

Her grandfather did not come to the bank. Since the night of the lamp he had spoken to her only to hear the history one last time, and when she reached the salt road he had closed his eyes and nodded, and said nothing at all.

They rode on a salt boat, long and narrow as a fallen palm, poled by four men who sang to keep time. Grey slabs of rock salt lay stacked in its belly, each one as long as Sira’s arm. The flood had turned the land into a sea of brown water and green rice, with villages standing on their mounds like islands.

On the third afternoon Djenné rose out of the water, a town of earth on its own island, every wall the colour of the riverbank.

Above the flat roofs stood the walls of the great mosque, and the call to prayer drifted out over the water as the boatmen leaned on their poles. Lamine whistled. Sira was looking at the crowd on the landing, and found she could not swallow.

# --- page 7
The market began at the water’s edge. Porters carried salt slabs up the bank on their heads, and traders sat behind them under awnings of matting, weighing gold dust on scales so small that a breath could tip them. There was cloth dyed deep indigo, dried fish in towers, kola nuts in baskets lined with leaves.

One porter was older than the rest. He was thin as a tent pole, and he walked bent under his slab with his eyes on the ground. A girl of about Sira’s age walked beside him with a water gourd, and each time he set the slab down, she held the gourd to his mouth.

Sira asked a woman selling fish who he was. The woman shrugged. “That is Fodé, old Yaya’s grandson. They were traders once, that family, or so people say. Now they carry other men’s salt.”

Sira stood very still among the baskets. The name had been in her mouth every day since her grandfather fell ill, and here it was, walking past her with a slab of salt on its head. The girl with the gourd glanced back at her once, curious, and then the crowd closed between them.

# --- page 8
Bakary’s house stood near the market, and its courtyard was bigger than Sira’s whole compound. By evening it was full. Guests sat on mats in their best white and indigo, and women in gold earrings carried bowls of rice and mutton among them. Lamps of clay burned in the niches of the walls, though the sky was still pale gold above them.

The baby had been given his name that morning. Now he slept on his mother’s lap, wrapped in white, while the grown-ups ate and laughed around him and Bakary sat on a raised seat at the far end, his robe embroidered all over with thread the colour of honey.

When the bowls were cleared, he beckoned Sira to him. He smiled for the guests and spoke so low that only she could hear. “Your grandfather and I understand each other. I hope you understand him too.”

“I know the whole history, sir,” said Sira. It was not quite an answer, but Bakary was already nodding to Lamine at the balafon, and the courtyard was going quiet, row by row, the way a field goes quiet when the wind drops.

# --- page 9
Sira had sung in her grandfather’s courtyard a thousand times. She had never sung to two hundred strangers. Her first line came out thin, and she saw a man near the front lean to whisper to his neighbour. Then Lamine’s balafon came in under her, steady as a pestle in a mortar, and her voice found the ground.

She sang Bakary’s family from the beginning. The hunter who first cleared land by the river. The woman who stood in her doorway and turned back raiders with a single word. The well, the herds, the first boat, the first gold. She sang the names seven generations deep, and not one was missing.

The guests began to call out. Old men cried the names back to her. A woman rose and dropped a string of cowrie shells at her feet, and another, and another. Bakary leaned back on his cushions and beamed.

“Bakary, son of Seku!”

Line by line, she sang the family forward, out of the old days and down the years, nearer and nearer to the grandfather of the man on the cushions. To Dramane. To the desert. To the salt road.

# --- page 10
Then her voice stopped. Lamine played on for a few notes, waiting for her, then lifted his beaters and held them in the air.

The silence spread from the front of the courtyard to the back. Somewhere a bowl knocked against a bowl, and someone hushed it. The baby stirred in his mother’s lap. Two hundred faces turned to Sira, and Sira stood in the middle of them with her mouth open and nothing coming out of it.

Bakary’s smile did not move, but his hand closed on his knee. Sira knew what came next in the history. She knew what came next if she skipped it: one easy breath, a jump of seventy years, a song about the new baby, and the gold that was already paid. She thought of her grandfather with his face to the wall.

And then, past all the faces, she saw the gate. Someone stood just outside it in the last light, where porters and servants waited for what was left of the feast: a thin old man, bent as if he still carried something, and a girl beside him with a water gourd. They were listening.

# The Truth

# --- page 11
Sira took a breath that went all the way down to her feet, and sang.

She sang Dramane and Yaya, two young men with one string of camels, going north to the salt mines in the empty country. She sang the wind that turned the sky brown at noon and buried their camp in a single night. She sang Yaya digging with his hands until his fingers were raw, and dragging his friend to a well.

Then she sang the salt sold for gold in Timbuktu, and the gold carried home in one man’s saddlebags. She sang the share that was never paid. She sang seventy years of Yaya’s children carrying other men’s salt.

Nobody called out now. Nobody moved. Somewhere along the way Lamine’s balafon had crept back in beneath her, very softly, and when she stopped, it stopped too.

Bakary was on his feet. His great robe swung about him, and his voice, which had been so soft, rang off the earth walls. “Enough! Your grandfather was paid. You were paid!” The baby woke and began to wail.

# --- page 12
Sira’s knees were shaking so hard she was sure the whole courtyard could see them through her wrap. She pressed her hands flat against her sides to keep them still.

“My grandfather was paid,” she said. Her voice came out small, and she made it bigger. “But a jeli is paid to remember, not to forget. That is the whole of the work. If I sing your family with a hole in the middle, it is not your history any more. It is a wall with no bricks at the bottom.”

Bakary said nothing. His jaw was working as if he were chewing something hard. Sira looked past him at the gate, where the old porter had not moved, and then back at Bakary’s face.

“Dramane was your grandfather,” she said. “What he did is not your shame. You were not born. But the share is still unpaid, and it is still in your house. If you leave it there, that part will be yours.”

# --- page 13
For a long moment nobody spoke. Then a man rose from the mat nearest the raised seat. It was Bakary’s son, the baby’s father, a young man with a quiet face who had not said ten words all evening. He looked at his father. He looked at the gate.

“My son was given his name today,” he said. “I want him to have all of his history, not most of it.”

He walked the length of the courtyard between the mats, and the guests leaned aside to let him through. At the gate he took the old porter by both hands, as a man greets an elder, and led him in. The girl with the gourd came behind them, holding it against her chest.

He sat Fodé down on his own mat. Then he turned to his father. “The share,” he said. “Will you pay it, or shall I?”

Bakary did not answer for a long time. He looked old suddenly, and very tired, and still angry. At last he lifted one heavy hand. “I will pay it,” he said, not looking at anyone. “And the jeli’s family will never sing for mine again.”

# --- page 14
Four days later, at dawn, a fisherman’s boat put Sira and Lamine ashore below the compound. Her grandfather was sitting on the bank in his white robe, as if he had been sitting there all night.

She sat down beside him and told him all of it: the market, the gate, the son, the share. When she came to the last thing Bakary had said, she stopped and looked at her hands.

Her grandfather was quiet for a while. Then he took the bundle of white cotton from inside his robe, knotted again round the gold, and laid it in her lap. “Take it back to him,” he rasped. “I took it because I am old, and I was afraid of being poor. I sent you because I hoped you were not afraid yet.”

“I was,” said Sira. “The whole time.” He laughed then, a cracked sound like a dry gourd, and put his hand on her shoulder.

They sat and watched the river go pink, then gold. Out on the water a fisherman was singing as he poled, and the weaverbirds were shouting in the reeds. After a while Sira began to sing too, quietly, the names of Bakary’s family, every one of them, from the beginning to the end.
