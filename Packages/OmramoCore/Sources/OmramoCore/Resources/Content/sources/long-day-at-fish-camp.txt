# Part I

# --- page 1
At fish camp in June, the day never really ends.

The sun rolled low around the sky, and the river shone like the side of a fish. Smoke slipped out of the smokehouse, sweet and sharp.

Under a blue tarp hung the drying racks, row after row of red salmon strips, turning a little in the wind.

At the cutting table stood Grandma with her uluaq, the curved knife she used for fish. Flick, flick it went, and a salmon lay open like a book.

Nick was nine, and his job was to carry. He took the strips to the racks, one bucket at a time.

# --- page 2
For three summers Nick had helped with the nets. He had held the rope and bailed the skiff. But the fish had always been somebody else’s.

Tonight Uncle Joe cut the motor out on the wide brown Kuskokwim. He nodded at the short net, strung between its white floats.

“This one is yours,” he said. “I’ll just keep the boat straight.”

Nick pulled, hand over hand. The net came up dripping, empty, empty. Then it pulled back.

A weight. A silver flash. Thump! Over the side slid a king salmon, nearly as long as his arm.

# --- page 3
Nick carried the fish up the muddy bank himself, hugging it with both arms. Nobody was allowed to help.

His cousins came running. His aunt whistled. Even the dogs stood up to look.

Nick could see it all already. His strips on the rack, the reddest in the row. His name on the lid of a jar. All winter he would eat that fish, and tell everyone at the table who had caught it.

He laid it on the cutting table, right in front of Grandma.

# --- page 4
Grandma wiped her uluaq on her apron and looked the fish over from nose to tail. “That is a fine fish,” she said. “Whose is it?”

“Mine!” said Nick.

“Mm. And where does a first catch go?”

Nick knew. Last summer his cousin Mary had carried her first fish to the neighbours, and the summer before, so had her brother. Nick had watched them go and thought it a fine thing to do. It had not been his fish then.

He looked down at his rubber boots.

# --- page 5
“Could I give away the second one?” he asked. “I’ll catch a second one tomorrow. A bigger one.”

“The second one is never the first one,” said Grandma. She was not cross. She was already pulling long wet grass from the bank.

“Take it to Lena, downriver. Her knees are keeping her off the water this summer, and she has no one to pull a net for her.”

She wrapped the salmon in the grass, cool and green, and put the bundle in his arms. It was heavier than before.

Nick did not say yes. But he started walking.

# Part II

# --- page 6
The path to Lena’s cabin ran along the top of the bank.

Tall grass brushed his rain pants. Fireweed stood in it, just turning pink, and mosquitoes sang round his ears. He could not slap them. Both arms were full of fish.

Beside him the river slid by, wide as a lake and brown as tea.

He practised what to say. “This is for you, because of your knees.” No. “Grandma made me bring this.” Worse.

It was eleven at night. The sun still sat above the flat green tundra, and the whole sky was the colour of a peach.

# --- page 7
Lena’s cabin was small and grey, with one short rack beside it. Old Lena sat on the step with a mug of tea, watching the river.

Nick walked up to her and held out the bundle. Every word he had practised flew away, all but three.

“It’s my first.”

Lena put down her tea. She folded back the grass and took the salmon in both hands, as carefully as if it were much heavier than it was.

“Quyana,” she said. It was the Yup’ik word for thank you, and she said it slowly. Then she looked at the fish for a long time.

# --- page 8
Then she got up, slow on her knees, and carried the salmon to her table by the water. “Come and watch. My mother showed me this cut.”

Her uluaq rocked once behind the head and slid along the backbone. Two red sides fell open.

“Strips for my rack. The head for soup. The eggs go to my sister, the belly to the Charles family, and the scraps to Peter’s dogs.” She counted on her fingers. “Your one fish is going to four houses, Nick.”

She rinsed the knife. “My mother used to say the fish know a hand that gives. They come to it again.”

# --- page 9
Afterwards they sat on the step. Lena gave him a bowl of akutaq, last summer’s blueberries whipped soft and sweet with fat.

“Your grandfather was smaller than you when he caught his first,” she said. “He brought it to my mother. She grew up in a sod house, my mother, and she knew a good fish. He dropped it twice on the way, she told me.”

Nick laughed with his mouth full.

When he stood to go, she put a dry red strip from her own rack in his hand. That would be gone before he got home. The story about his grandfather would not.

# --- page 10
Nick walked home chewing. It was nearly midnight, and the sun was only now sliding down to the edge of the tundra. His arms were empty. They swung at his sides, and he felt a little taller.

Grandma was still at her table. “Well?” she asked. “She said quyana,” said Nick.

“And what did you say?” Nick grinned. “Tomorrow, can I pull the net again?”

“One more day,” said Grandma. “When the racks are full, we stop.”

Behind her the racks hung red in the wind, the smoke climbed into the gold, and the long day went on.
