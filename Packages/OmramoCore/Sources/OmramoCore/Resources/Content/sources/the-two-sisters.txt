# The Bridge

# --- page 1
If you cross the Lions Gate Bridge on a clear morning, look up.

Below you, the sea runs fast and green through a narrow gap between two shores. A ship as long as a street slides under the bridge, stacked with steel boxes in red and blue, and its wake spreads behind it in a long white fan. Gulls hang in the wind beside the railing, barely moving their wings, and the air smells of salt and wet cedar.

At the south end of the bridge, the dark cedars of Stanley Park crowd down to the water. Beyond them, the glass towers of the city stand pale in the mist. At the north end, the mountains of the North Shore rise so steeply that they seem to start in the sea itself.

Lift your eyes above the nearest ridges, above the dark firs, to where the snow begins. There, high and far off, two rounded peaks stand side by side against the sky, so close together that from here they look like two people sitting shoulder to shoulder.

# --- page 2
People in the city call those peaks The Lions. Most of the drivers rushing over the bridge this morning, coffee cups wedged beside them, know them by no other name, if they notice them at all.

But the peaks had a name long before there was a city here, or a bridge, or a single road. The Sḵwx̱wú7mesh, whose people have lived around these waters for thousands of years, call them the Two Sisters.

The Sḵwx̱wú7mesh Nation lives here still. Its homes stand near the north end of this bridge, on the shores of Burrard Inlet, and to the north beside Howe Sound and the Squamish River. Its people still look up at the same two peaks you are looking at now.

There is a story about how the sisters came to be up there. It is not a story about lions. It is about a war, and a feast, and two girls who asked their father for the one gift he least wanted to give.

# --- page 3
The story reached English readers through two friends. One was Chief Joe Capilano, a leader of the Sḵwx̱wú7mesh, who had once crossed an ocean to speak to the king in London on behalf of his people. The other was Pauline Johnson, a Mohawk poet from far in the east, who had come to live in Vancouver.

More than a hundred years ago, before this bridge was built, the two of them walked together on the North Shore. The chief looked up at the peaks and told her what his people say about them. She listened, and later she wrote it down, and in 1911 it was printed in a book.

Every telling of a story is a little different. A teller chooses where to begin, and what to leave in the quiet between the words.

This is our telling, written with care for the people whose story it is. It begins long before the city, when the only roads on this water were the wakes of canoes, and the only lights on the shore were cooking fires.

# --- page 4
Long ago, there was no bridge, and no city, and no ship as long as a street. The same green water ran through the narrows. The same mountains stood behind, furred with fir and cedar to the snowline.

Along the shores stood villages of long houses built from cedar planks, their roofs sloping down toward the hills and their fronts facing the sea. Smoke rose from them in the still mornings. Before each village lay a beach of grey pebbles, and on it, in rows, the canoes were drawn up out of reach of the tide.

The year turned on the sea and the rivers. In spring the herring came, so many that the water shivered with them. Then came the berries on the hillsides. And at the end of summer the salmon came home from the ocean, crowding up the rivers until the shallows were more fish than water.

On one of those shores, in the largest of the cedar houses, lived a great chief whose name was known all along the coast. His canoes were the fastest on the inlet, and in all his years he had never lost a fight.

# --- page 5
For as long as anyone could remember, his people had been at war with peoples of the north coast, many days’ paddling away. The war had no beginning that anyone living had seen. It was simply there, like weather.

In some summers the long northern canoes came south, riding the tide in through the narrows before dawn. In others the chief’s own canoes went north to answer them. Either way, the young men went, and not all of them came home, and the houses where they had lived fell quiet.

On summer nights the mothers of the village slept lightly. They listened through the cedar walls for the dip and knock of paddles out on the dark water, and when they heard it, they lay very still until they knew whose canoes they were.

The chief was not afraid of the northern peoples. He had beaten them too often for that. He spoke of them with scorn, and his warriors laughed when he did.

But in his house, through all those years of war, his two daughters were growing up. They did everything together: they gathered berries side by side, paddled one canoe, and laughed at the same jokes, until the village rarely spoke of one without the other.

# The Sisters’ Wish

# --- page 6
In the same spring, both of the chief’s daughters came of age.

It was the season when the first fern shoots were uncurling on the forest floor. The chief meant the whole coast to know how proud he was of them. There would be a great feast for their coming of age, in the custom of their people, and guests would come from all along the coast.

For a whole moon the village made ready. Canoes went out and came back low in the water with fish. On the river flats people dug roots and dried berries in the sun, and the weavers made new cedar-bark mats for the guests to sit on.

Only one thing was left as it was. The war went on. The chief told his warriors to put away their weapons until the feast was over, the way a man sets down a heavy load to rest his arms, meaning to pick it up again.

Seven days before the feast, the two sisters came to find their father. He was down on the beach, looking over the canoes. They came across the pebbles together, their shoulders touching, and he saw from their faces that they had come to ask him something.

# --- page 7
The chief straightened and smiled. There was nothing in the world he would refuse these two, and he wanted them to know it.

“You have a favour to ask,” he said. “Then ask it, and do not be afraid. I have never once refused you two, and I will not begin today. Name the gift, and it is yours: blankets of mountain-goat wool, a canoe carved for you alone.”

The sisters looked at each other. One of them spoke, but it was clear the words belonged to both of them, and that they had been carrying them for a long time.

“We do not want blankets or canoes, Father,” she said. “We want guests. Invite the peoples of the north to our feast. Not as enemies. As guests, to sit on our mats and eat our food and see us honoured.”

On the beach behind him, a warrior mending a paddle stopped with his knife in the air. Gulls went on crying over the water. The chief said nothing at all. The smile had gone from his face, and in its place was something his daughters had never seen there before.

# --- page 8
He was silent for so long that the tide crept up to his feet and began to wet the pebbles around them. His daughters waited. They did not take back a single word.

He was thinking of many things at once. A chief does not lightly send the first canoe toward an enemy. If he invited the northern peoples and they came, would they come as guests, or as a war party with a feast for an excuse? If he invited them and they laughed, every village on the coast would hear that the great chief had begged for peace.

Behind him, his warriors were muttering. They had buried brothers in this war. Some of them had fathers who had never come home from the north.

But he had already said yes. He had said it before he knew what they would ask, and a chief who takes back his word to his own daughters is not a chief anyone should follow.

At last he lifted his head. “Choose our best paddlers,” he said. “Tomorrow they take two canoes up the coast, and they take no weapons. My daughters have asked for guests, and guests they shall have.”

# --- page 9
The young men who went north were the best paddlers in the village. They went at first light in two long cedar canoes, each one carved from the trunk of a single tree, and the whole village came down to the beach to watch them go.

They carried no spears. They carried the chief’s words, which were simple: the daughters of the chief were to be honoured, and the peoples of the north were asked to come and eat at his fire.

Fires were kept burning day and night on the points of land near the village, so that anyone paddling south would see the smoke and know it for a welcome.

Then the waiting began. Each morning the sisters climbed the rocks above the beach and watched the place where the water met the sky. Each evening they came down again with nothing to tell. The warriors watched too, for different reasons, and they kept their weapons where they could reach them.

On the sixth morning, one of the sisters caught the other’s arm and pointed. Far out, where the inlet opened to the sea, a line of dark marks had appeared on the water, and the line was growing longer.

# --- page 10
They were canoes, more than anyone had ever seen together on that water, and they came on steadily with the tide. In the village, the warriors gathered at the top of the beach. Nobody spoke. The chief stood at the water’s edge with his daughters beside him and waited, his hands empty.

As the first canoes came close, the people on the beach began to make out who was in them. There were paddlers, and there were warriors. But there were also women sitting among the bundles, and old men wrapped in blankets, and children, small ones, leaning over the sides to look at the strange shore.

A murmur ran along the beach, and then the warriors’ hands loosened. No one brings their children to a war. The northern peoples had understood the invitation exactly as it was meant, and they had answered it in the only way that could not be mistaken.

The canoes grounded one after another on the pebbles. The northern chiefs stepped ashore with gifts in their arms, dried fish and cedar boxes from their own country. The chief walked down to meet them, and the feast began.

# The Peaks

# --- page 11
The feast lasted for many days, and no one who was there ever forgot it.

There was salmon roasted on cedar stakes beside the fires, and berries, and roots, and more food than the guests could eat. By day there were songs and games on the beach. Gifts went from hand to hand. Old enemies sat on the same cedar mats and passed the same bowls, and slowly, carefully, they began to talk.

Every person there was a witness. Each one had eaten the chief’s food and heard his words, and on this coast, what is said before so many witnesses cannot afterwards be unsaid.

On the last day, the chief stood up before them all, and the northern chiefs stood up to face him. They spoke for a long time, one after another. When they had finished, the war that no one living had seen begin was over.

When the guests at last paddled north again, they went as friends, and friends they stayed. In the summers after that, the long canoes still went up and down the coast. But they carried fishing nets, and trade goods, and visiting families, and never again a war party.

# --- page 12
And the two sisters? The story says that what they had done was not forgotten. Nobody before them had thought to ask for peace as a gift. Nobody had believed it could be asked for at all.

So, the story says, the sisters were raised up onto the mountains above the inlet, side by side. From there they could see the whole coast at once: the villages, the narrows, the canoes going to and fro on the water. There they were to stay for ever, two sisters looking down on the peace they had made.

Snow gathers on their shoulders in winter and melts away in summer. Clouds settle over them and lift again. Eagles turn in the air below them. They do not move.

Ever since, the people of the inlet have looked up at the two peaks white against the sky and remembered the feast, the canoes full of children, and the two girls who asked.

And then, much later, other people came to the inlet, in ships with tall sails, from the far side of the world. They looked up at the peaks too, and they saw something quite different.

# --- page 13
The settlers thought the two rounded summits looked like a pair of great lions, lying side by side. So that is what they called them: The Lions. They built a city on the shore below, with streets and railways and a harbour full of ships, and the city grew until it filled the land between the water and the hills. Some of it was built where villages of the coast peoples had stood.

In 1938 they finished a bridge across the narrows, so that people could drive to new houses on the North Shore. They named it for the peaks. At its south end, two lions made of concrete were set on either side of the road, crouched and watchful.

The bridge is busy from dawn until long after dark. Most of the people on it could tell you the name of the peaks above them. Not many could tell you there is a second name, with a story folded up inside it.

Now you are one of the ones who know. Next time you cross and the mist lifts off the mountains, you may still see two lions lying in the snow. But you will see two sisters too, sitting close together, looking down at the water.

# --- page 14
Come back to the bridge in the evening, when the traffic thins and the water in the narrows turns the colour of pewter. The ships have their lights on now. Along the shore, one window after another begins to glow.

The city goes into shadow first. Then the forest on the lower slopes, then the ridges, one by one, as if someone were drawing a blanket slowly up the mountains.

Only the two peaks are still clear of shadow. A thin cloud that has hidden them all afternoon slides away, and there they are, side by side, close enough to touch, small and white above the bridge and the towers and the dark water.

Somewhere below them a canoe is coming home across the inlet, its paddles dipping in time. Somewhere a child is looking up.

The snow fades to grey and then to blue, as slowly as embers cooling on a hearth. The first stars come out above the sisters. But they will be there in the morning, as they have been every morning since the feast, side by side above the water where the war ended.
