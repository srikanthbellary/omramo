# - Ozaki's old man finds her in the morning and carries her in his hand; ours finds her at dusk

# - The guards who cannot lift their arms, the robe of feathers that takes away the sorrows of the

# - Our men burn the letter and pour the water of life on the fire; Ozaki's burn it at sunrise.

# Part I

# --- page 1
An old man in Japan once cut bamboo for his living.

From the tall green stems he and his wife made baskets and ladles and flutes to sell, and people called him the Bamboo-Cutter.

One evening, when the grove was already dark, he saw one stem that shone. Light glowed through its green skin like a lamp through paper.

He cut it open with his knife, as gently as he could. In the hollow sat a girl no longer than his hand, looking up at him.

“I have cut bamboo all my life,” he whispered, “and never found a daughter in it before.”

# --- page 2
He carried her home in his sleeve. His wife lined a basket with soft cloth, and there the tiny girl slept.

After that day the old man’s luck changed. Whenever he split a stem of bamboo, gold pieces lay shining inside it.

The child grew as fast as bamboo itself. In three months she was a tall young woman with long black hair, and a pale light came from her. No room she sat in was ever dark.

So she was given the name Kaguya-hime, the Shining Princess.

On the blackest night, the old woman could thread her needle at her daughter’s side.

# --- page 3
Word of the Shining Princess travelled all the way to the capital. Soon great lords in ox-carts crowded the lane.

They waited at the fence through rain and snow. One by one they grew cold and went home, until only five were left. Those five would not go.

“I am old, my child,” said the Bamboo-Cutter. “Choose one of them, so that you are not left alone.”

Kaguya-hime did not wish to marry anyone. “Then let each bring me one thing,” she said, “a thing no one has ever brought. I will marry the man who truly does it.”

# --- page 4
The first lord had to find a stone bowl that shines by itself. He brought a sooty one from a temple down the road, and it did not shine at all.

The second had to find a branch of gold with jewels for fruit. His looked perfect, until six craftsmen knocked at the door. “Please, lady, he has not paid us for making it!”

The third had to find a robe of fire-rat fur, which no flame can burn. Kaguya-hime laid it on the coals. Puff! It was gone.

The last two lords heard about it and quietly gave up.

# --- page 5
Then the Emperor himself came riding to the house among the bamboo.

He saw her shining in the dim room. “Come to my palace,” he said. “You shall be the first lady of the land.”

“I cannot,” said Kaguya-hime. “If I am forced, I shall vanish like a shadow.” And already her edges were growing faint.

The Emperor could command anyone in Japan. He looked at her for a long moment, then bowed and rode home alone.

But he sent her a poem, and she sent one back. For three years their poems flew to and fro like birds.

# Part II

# --- page 6
In the fourth autumn, Kaguya-hime began to watch the moon.

Night after night she sat on the porch and looked up. It was the season when families sat out to admire the moon, but the rounder it grew, the more she wept.

“What is it, my child?” asked the old man. “What do you see up there?”

“My first home,” she said. “I was not born in this world. I came from the Moon. On the night of the mid-autumn full moon, my people will come to fetch me.”

She held his rough hand in both of hers. “I would stay if I could.”

# --- page 7
The Emperor sent two thousand guards with bows. A thousand stood on the roof, and a thousand ringed the wall.

The old man barred every door. He sat in the inmost room with his arms around his daughter. “Nobody shall take you,” he said.

At midnight the sky turned brighter than ten full moons together. Down from the Moon came a cloud, and on it stood tall, calm people who shone.

The guards lifted their bows, and their arms fell slack. Not one arrow flew. Then, with no hand upon them, the barred doors slid open, one after another.

# --- page 8
Kaguya-hime stepped out into the light. She took off her outer robe and put it into her mother’s hands.

“Keep this,” she said. “And when the moon is bright, look up. I shall be looking down.”

Then she wrote a letter to the Emperor and set a small jar beside it. Whoever drank from that jar would never die.

A Moon woman held up a robe of feathers. Whoever wore it forgot the sorrows of the earth, and its loves as well.

It settled on her shoulders, and her tears stopped. She stepped onto the cloud, and the cloud rose, grew small, and was gone.

# --- page 9
The old couple folded the robe and laid it away. On bright nights they sat on the porch together and looked up.

The letter and the jar were carried to the palace. The Emperor read the letter twice. He did not open the jar.

“What use is living for ever,” he said, “if I never see her again?”

He sat silent for a long time. Then he called his ministers. “Tell me,” he said, “which mountain in my land stands nearest to the sky?”

“There is one in the east, my lord,” they answered, “so high that its head is white with snow.”

# --- page 10
So the Emperor’s men carried the letter and the jar up past the trees and past the clouds, to the very top.

There, under the setting moon, they lit a fire. They burned the letter and poured the water of life on the flames, and not one man tasted a drop.

The smoke rose in one thin thread, straight toward the Moon.

That mountain is Mount Fuji. People said the smoke went on rising for many, many years.

And sometimes, on a clear night, a wisp of cloud still trails from its top, like a letter on its way.
